① Intuition"With Everything Else Equal"

How much does loneliness predict "on top of" everything else?

You predict the T2 depression total from the T1 loneliness total and get a slope of 0.32: for each 1 point higher on T1 loneliness, T2 depression is 0.32 points higher on average. Your supervisor presses: "Lonely people also tend to sleep badly and have less social support. How much of that 0.32 really belongs to sleep and social support? And people who are depressed at T1 are mostly still depressed at T2, and people depressed at T1 also tend to be lonely. Could what you see just be 'already depressed'?" To answer these questions, you need to put several predictors into the model at once: {{T:multiple regression|}}.

Partial regression coefficients: "holding the other variables constant"

Multiple regression gives each predictor a coefficient, called a {{T:partial regression coefficient|}}. It means: comparing people who are the same on all the other predictors, how much the outcome differs on average when this variable is 1 unit higher. The variables put into the model to be "held constant" are called {{T:covariates|}} (control variables).

Analogy: compare only people in the same situation

Imagine sorting the 800 people into many small groups by sleep duration and social support, so that within each group everyone has almost the same sleep and social support. Look at the relationship between loneliness and T2 depression inside each group, then combine the results across groups: that is the coefficient of loneliness "after controlling for sleep and social support." Multiple regression does this in one step with mathematics, without actually forming groups.

In the course data (R/L3-2.R), the coefficient of loneliness changes from model to model:

ModelPredictors of T2 depressionCoefficient of T1 loneliness
m1T1 loneliness only0.324
m2T1 loneliness + social support + sleep duration0.282
m3T1 loneliness + T1 depression0.140

Adding social support and sleep makes the coefficient only slightly smaller; adding T1 depression cuts it to less than half. The explanation: T1 loneliness is correlated with T1 depression (r = 0.31), and T1 depression is by far the strongest predictor of T2 depression (people's depression is fairly stable). In m1, the coefficient of loneliness "borrowed" part of the predictive power that belongs to T1 depression; m3 gives it back. The 0.14 that remains is what loneliness adds in predicting T2 depression "among people with the same T1 depression."

Why does a coefficient change after controlling?

The simulation above shows a rule (all variables standardized):

$$\text{coefficient of loneliness in simple regression} = \text{unique effect of loneliness} + \text{effect of sleep} \times \rho(\text{loneliness},\text{sleep})$$

If you leave out a variable that is correlated with loneliness and also affects depression (here, sleep problems), the coefficient of loneliness "absorbs" part of its effect. This is called {{T:omitted variable bias|}}, the regression version of "confounding" from Lesson 1.4. Put the variable into the model (control for it) and this part of the bias disappears. Drag ρ to 0 and the two coefficients become equal: if two predictors are uncorrelated, controlling for one does not change the other's coefficient.

More controls is not always better

"Controlling" handles only the variables you measured and put into the model; unmeasured confounders are still there. Also, if you control for a variable in the middle of a causal chain (for example loneliness → worse sleep → depression, and you control for sleep), you also "control away" the part of loneliness's effect that runs through sleep; a smaller coefficient then does not mean loneliness is unimportant. What to control for should follow from the research question and theory, not from putting in every variable you can find.

The link to CLPN: one edge = one coefficient that controls for all other T1 variables

Model m3 is a "mini CLPM":

$$\text{depression}_{T2} = b_0 + \underbrace{0.534}_{\text{autoregressive}}\times\text{depression}_{T1} + \underbrace{0.140}_{\text{cross-lagged}}\times\text{loneliness}_{T1}$$

CLPN scales this up to the item level: when predicting T2 Depressed mood, for example, all 9 T1 items go on the right-hand side at once (including T1 Depressed mood itself). So the edge "T1 Feeling isolated → T2 Depressed mood" means: among people who are the same at T1 on the other 8 items (including Depressed mood itself), how much higher T2 Depressed mood is on average when Feeling isolated is 1 unit higher. This property of "controlling for all other T1 nodes" is the basic difference between a CLPN edge and a simple correlation: it removes confounding by the other measured T1 items.

That does not make CLPN edges "clean." One kind of confounding cannot be removed however many T1 items you control for: each person's long-term {{T:stable trait|}}. Some people, for instance, are consistently lonelier than others and also consistently more prone to low mood. Such stable individual differences make "people high on T1 loneliness are also high on T2 depression" true even if, within one person, rises and falls in loneliness are not followed by rises and falls in depression. The T1 items are only an imperfect measure of this trait; putting them into the regression absorbs only part of the trait's influence, and the rest still leaks into the cross-lagged coefficients. The course data were generated exactly this way: data/true_B_within.csv is the within-person effect used to generate the data, and data/true_B_population.csv is what a standard CLPN actually estimates in the population (the population target). The two tables differ, and the difference comes from the stable traits. Lesson 5.3 (RI-CLPM) shows how to separate between-person and within-person variation.

Multicollinearity: when two predictors are nearly the same

When two predictors are highly correlated, the model struggles to tell "whose credit it is." This is called {{T:multicollinearity|}}. The coefficients are not necessarily biased, but they become very unstable: the standard errors grow, and the coefficients can swing up and down or even change sign. An extreme example (R code below): copy the loneliness total, add a tiny bit of noise (correlation 0.995), and put both into the model. The coefficient of loneliness goes from 0.32 to 1.28, the copy gets −0.96, and the standard error balloons from 0.04 to 0.37. The sum of the two coefficients (≈ 0.32) does not change: the model knows the total contribution of "loneliness-type stuff" but cannot split it between two almost identical variables.

The usual diagnostic is the variance inflation factor, VIF (see the math section). As a rule of thumb, VIF > 5 or 10 deserves attention (the cutoff differs between textbooks). In m2 on the course data, the VIF of loneliness is ≈ 1.23, no problem. In a CLPN, items often have similar content and are highly correlated with each other; that is one reason LASSO regularization helps (Lesson 3.5), and one reason node selection needs care (Lesson 9.2).

Interactions (a brief introduction)

An {{T:interaction|}} means that the effect of one variable depends on the level of another. For example, how strongly loneliness predicts depression may differ between men and women. In R you write y ~ x1 * x2 (the same as x1 + x2 + x1:x2), and the coefficient of the interaction term says "how much the slope of x1 changes for each 1-unit increase in x2." A standard CLPN has no interaction terms; here you only need to know the concept exists.

② Essential MathThe Multiple Regression Equation and VIF

$$\hat y = b_0 + b_1x_1 + b_2x_2 + \cdots + b_px_p$$
\(x_1,\dots,x_p\)
The \(p\) predictors.
\(b_j\)
The partial regression coefficient of predictor \(j\): with the other \(x\) fixed, each 1-unit increase in \(x_j\) changes \(\hat y\) by \(b_j\). In calculus terms, \(b_j = \partial \hat y / \partial x_j\), exactly the partial derivative that "moves only one variable."

It is solved the same way as simple regression: make \(\sum (y_i-\hat y_i)^2\) as small as possible by setting the partial derivative with respect to each \(b_j\) to 0, which gives \(p+1\) equations (solved at once with matrices; R does it for you).

Another way to see "controlling": remove the overlap first, then regress

The Frisch–Waugh theorem: \(b_1\) equals the result of two steps. (1) Predict \(x_1\) from the other predictors and keep the residuals (the part of \(x_1\) the other variables cannot explain); (2) predict \(y\) from these residuals. So a partial regression coefficient uses only the "unique part" of \(x_1\). In the course data, predicting T2 depression from "the residuals of T1 loneliness regressed on T1 depression" gives a slope of exactly 0.1398, identical to the coefficient of loneliness in m3.

The variance inflation factor

$$VIF_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2}$$

\(R_j^2\) is the R² from "predicting \(x_j\) from all the other predictors." The better the other variables explain \(x_j\), the smaller its unique part, the closer \(R_j^2\) is to 1, the larger the VIF, and the variance (SE²) of \(b_j\) is multiplied by the VIF. Example: with \(R_j^2 = 0.9\), VIF = 10, and the SE is \(\sqrt{10}\approx 3.2\) times larger.

SummaryThree Takeaways

  • A partial regression coefficient compares people who are the same on the other predictors; when predictors are correlated, adding a control variable changes the coefficient (omitted variable bias).
  • Multicollinearity makes coefficients unstable and SEs large; VIF = 1/(1 − R²ⱼ).
  • Each CLPN edge is a partial regression coefficient that controls for all other T1 nodes (including the autoregressive one). Next lesson: {{L:3.3}}.
