① Intuition"Which Factor Matters More?" Put Them on the Same Units First

Social support or sleep: which predicts depression more strongly?

Model m2 in Lesson 3.2 gave a coefficient of −0.252 for social support and −0.371 for sleep duration. Can you say "sleep matters more than social support"? Not directly. Social support is the mean of a 1–5 scale; sleep is in hours. One point more social support is a large difference (the standard deviation in the whole sample is only 0.79 points), and one more hour of sleep is also about 1 standard deviation (1.02 hours). Another example: if sleep were recorded in minutes, its coefficient would become −0.0062, a number 60 times smaller, yet the relationship between sleep and depression would not have changed at all.

A coefficient that carries the original units is called an {{T:unstandardized coefficient|}}: "for each 1 original unit more of x, y is b original units higher on average." Its size depends on the units, so you cannot compare it directly across variables measured in different units.

The fix: first turn every variable (including the outcome) into z-scores (Lesson 1.3), then run the regression. Every variable is now in units of "1 standard deviation," and the coefficients are called {{T:standardized regression coefficients (β)|}}: "for each 1 standard deviation higher on x, y is β standard deviations higher on average (with the other predictors unchanged)."

("Monthly income" in the widget is a made-up example; the course data have no income variable. It only shows how b and β change when the units change.)

β takes its unit from this sample

β uses "1 standard deviation" as a common unit, so predictors measured in different units can be compared. But it is the standard deviation of this sample: the same effect gives a larger β in a sample where sleep varies a lot than in one where everyone sleeps about the same. β is good for comparisons within one model; compare across samples with care.

Standardized, the results of m2 become: loneliness β = 0.26, sleep β = −0.11, social support β = −0.06 (not significant). Now you can say: in this sample and this model, T1 loneliness is the strongest unique predictor of T2 depression; sleep duration is weaker; and once the other two are controlled for, the unique prediction of social support is close to zero.

How to read β

"β = 0.25" reads: with the other predictors held constant, for each 1 standard deviation higher on x, y is 0.25 standard deviations higher on average. Don't read it as "x explains 25% of y" (that is R² language, and β² is not the unique share explained either, except when there is only one predictor), and don't read it as "x increases by 25%."

With a single predictor, β equals the correlation coefficient r. With several predictors, β is "partial": it is the value after controlling for the other variables, and it can differ a lot from r, even in sign.

When to report b, and when β?

Unstandardized bStandardized β
UnitOriginal units (points, hours, years)Standard deviations
Good forWhen the unit itself means something: "each extra hour of sleep goes with depression 0.37 points lower on average"; prediction; comparing the same scale across studiesComparing predictors in different units within one model; scale scores whose units have no intuitive meaning
DrawbackNot comparable across different unitsDepends on this sample's standard deviations: less variation in the sample, smaller β (restriction of range, Lesson 1.4); β from different samples may not be comparable

Many journals recommend reporting both, or reporting b with its confidence interval along with an effect size.

Why are CLPN edges usually based on standardized variables?

In a CLPN network plot, all edges are drawn in one figure, and line thickness is used to compare "which edge is stronger." Without standardizing, coefficients between items on different scales (loneliness items 1–4, depression items 0–3) are not comparable: the two kinds of items have different standard deviations, and items with larger standard deviations get smaller-looking coefficients (a different starting point alone would not affect a coefficient). So the usual practice is to standardize all items first (the course scripts' clpn_estimate() does exactly this). In addition, the LASSO in Lesson 3.5 penalizes the absolute size of the coefficients; if the variables have different units, the penalty is unfair, which is another reason to standardize first.

Note: an "edge weight" is still not an absolute measure of effect size

Standardized coefficients depend on the sample's variance structure; in different samples (for example a community sample vs. a clinical sample) the item variances differ, and β cannot be compared directly. Comparing networks between two groups needs dedicated methods (such as the Network Comparison Test), which this course does not cover.

Writing a proper results report

Most psychology and social work journals use {{T:APA style|}} (the Publication Manual of the American Psychological Association). A report of a regression result usually includes the direction and size of the effect (b or β), the 95% confidence interval, the test statistic or p-value, and the model's R². The main conventions:

  • Statistical symbols are italic (b, p, t, R²); the standardized coefficient is the Greek letter β (upright, not italic); the unstandardized coefficient is italic b (or B). Don't mix the two up.
  • Report exact p-values (such as p = .019), and write p < .001 below .001. Statistics that cannot exceed 1 (such as p and r, and β, which is usually below 1) are written without a 0 before the decimal point (APA convention).
  • State the relationship first, then give the statistics in parentheses; use "predicts" or "is associated with," and with observational data don't write "causes" or "affects."
  • Say what you controlled for.
An example (based on this lesson's R output)

A multiple linear regression was run with the T2 depression total as the outcome and T1 loneliness and T1 depression as predictors (all variables standardized). Controlling for T1 depression, T1 loneliness still positively predicted T2 depression, β = .13, 95% CI [.07, .19], p < .001; the autoregressive effect of T1 depression was strong, β = .54, 95% CI [.48, .60], p < .001. The model explained 35% of the variance in T2 depression (R² = .35).

In one APA-style sentence: Controlling for T1 depression, T1 loneliness positively predicted T2 depression, β = .13, 95% CI [.07, .19], p < .001.

Common reporting mistakes

  1. Reporting only stars, or only p: readers can't tell how large the effect is. A p-value only says "if the true effect were 0, how unlikely a result this extreme would be" (Lesson 1.6); it is not an effect size. With a large sample, even a tiny effect gets p < .001.
  2. "Significant" = "important": β = .05 with p = .01 is statistically significant, but may matter little in practice. What a social worker cares about is whether the difference is noticeable in clients' lives.
  3. Not significant = no relationship: p = .109 for social support says only that this sample cannot rule out 0; its 95% CI [−.13, .01] includes both "a small negative effect" and "almost zero." Write "no significant unique prediction was found," not "social support is unrelated to depression."
  4. Comparing the sizes of two β and concluding "A matters more than B": if the two coefficients' confidence intervals overlap a lot, the difference may be only sampling error; β is also affected by the correlations between predictors and by each one's measurement reliability (a less reliable variable's β is underestimated); and "matters more" also depends on practical things such as whether the variable can be changed by an intervention and at what cost.
  5. Not stating the control variables and the sample: the same variable gets different coefficients in different models (Lesson 3.2), so readers must know what you controlled for.
  6. Causal wording: "loneliness affects/causes depression" should become "loneliness predicts / is associated with …." Even a longitudinal cross-lagged coefficient is only "prediction that comes earlier in time" (Module 5 discusses this in detail).
Social work perspective: translating β for frontline colleagues

Colleagues at your agency may not know what a "standard deviation" is. You could put it like this: "Of two clients with the same depression at T1, the one whose loneliness was one 'typical gap' above average scored, half a year later, about 0.13 of a typical gap higher on depression. The effect is small; in this sample, the 95% CI does not include 0." For something more concrete, go back to the original units: "For each 1 point higher on the loneliness scale, depression half a year later was about 0.14 points higher on average (out of a maximum of 15)."

② Essential MathConverting Between b and β

$$\beta_j = b_j \times \frac{s_{x_j}}{s_y}$$
\(b_j\)
The unstandardized coefficient of predictor \(j\).
\(s_{x_j}\)
That predictor's sample standard deviation.
\(s_y\)
The outcome's sample standard deviation.
\(\beta_j\)
The standardized coefficient.

Why? \(x\) going up 1 standard deviation = going up \(s_x\) original units, so \(y\) goes up \(b\cdot s_x\) original units = \(b\cdot s_x / s_y\) standard deviations of \(y\).

Check: in m3, loneliness has \(b\) = 0.1398, \(s_{\text{loneliness}}\) = 3.082, \(s_{\text{T2 depression}}\) = 3.365, so β = 0.1398 × 3.082/3.365 ≈ 0.128, which rounds to 0.13, matching the output of L3-3.R.

Changing units: with sleep in minutes, \(s_x\) becomes 60 times larger and \(b\) becomes 1/60, so their product β stays the same.

Confidence interval

$$95\%\ CI \approx b \pm 1.96\times SE(b)$$

(With a large sample, the critical value of the \(t\) distribution is about 1.96; R's confint() uses the exact \(t\) value.) The interval excludes 0 ⇔ significant at the .05 level.

③ R CodeStandardizing, Confidence Intervals, and Reporting

Script for this lesson: R/L3-3.R.

```r R/L3-3.R (core part) source("R/_setup.R") dat <- read_course_data() d <- data.frame(dep2 = rowSums(dat[, paste0(dep_items, "_t2")]), lone1 = rowSums(dat[, paste0(lone_items, "_t1")]), dep1 = rowSums(dat[, paste0(dep_items, "_t1")])) dz <- as.data.frame(scale(d)) # standardize every variable first → the coefficients are β fit <- lm(dep2 ~ lone1 + dep1, data = dz) ci <- confint(fit) # 95% confidence intervals s <- summary(fit)$coefficients for (v in c("lone1", "dep1")) { cat(sprintf("%s: β = %.2f, 95%% CI [%.2f, %.2f], p %s\n", v, s[v, 1], ci[v, 1], ci[v, 2], ifelse(s[v, 4] < .001, "< .001", sprintf("= %.3f", s[v, 4])))) } ``` ```out Output (R/output/L3-3_log.txt) lone1: β = 0.13, 95% CI [0.07, 0.19], p < .001 dep1: β = 0.54, 95% CI [0.48, 0.60], p < .001 ```

Notes: scale() returns a matrix, so as.data.frame() turns it back into a data frame; sprintf() formats numbers according to a template, where %.2f means two decimals and %% prints a percent sign.

```r Comparing b and β in m2 (predictors in different units) d$support <- dat$social_support_t1 d$sleep <- dat$sleep_hours_t1 f_b <- lm(dep2 ~ lone1 + support + sleep, data = d) f_beta <- lm(dep2 ~ lone1 + support + sleep, data = as.data.frame(scale(d))) round(cbind(b = coef(f_b), beta = coef(f_beta)), 3) ``` ```out Output (R/output/L3-3_log.txt, excerpt) b beta (Intercept) 4.672 0.000 lone1 0.282 0.258 support -0.252 -0.059 sleep -0.371 -0.112 ```

After standardizing, the intercept is 0 (every variable has mean 0). The b for social support (−0.252) and for sleep (−0.371) look similar, but as β the point estimate for sleep (−0.112) is about twice that for social support (−0.059). You can only say "the point estimate is about twice as large," though: as the exercise answer shows, their 95% CIs are [−0.18, −0.05] and [−0.13, 0.01], which overlap a lot. Whether two β really differ needs a test of the difference itself (for example, comparing a constrained and an unconstrained model); the ratio of two point estimates is not enough.

```r β by hand, and a change-of-units experiment round(coef(f_b)["lone1"] * sd(d$lone1) / sd(d$dep2), 3) # equals the β in the table above d$sleep_min <- d$sleep * 60 # sleep in minutes round(coef(lm(dep2 ~ lone1 + support + sleep_min, data = d))["sleep_min"], 4) ``` ```out Output (R/output/L3-3_log.txt, end) lone1 0.258 sleep_min -0.0062 ```

The hand-computed 0.258 is the same as the β in the table. With sleep in minutes, b goes from −0.371 to −0.0062 (= −0.371 / 60): a unit 60 times smaller makes the coefficient 60 times smaller, but the model's fit, t value, and p-value don't change, and neither does β. That is what "the size of b depends on the units" means.

Exercise 3.3-B: By hand, and interpretation

(1) Use β = b·sx/sy to compute β for T1 depression from m3 (unstandardized; b = 0.534, sT1 depression = 3.398, sT2 depression = 3.365). (2) Someone says, "gender has β = 0.12, so women are 0.12 standard deviations more depressed than men." Is that right?

Reference answer

(1) 0.534 × 3.398 / 3.365 ≈ 0.539, matching the output 0.54. (2) Not quite: β is the effect of "the gender variable being 1 standard deviation higher," and a 0/1 variable has a standard deviation of about 0.5, so "women vs. men (1 original unit apart)" is about β / sgender ≈ 0.12/0.5 = 0.24 standard deviations of y. For a binary variable, it is clearer to standardize only y and not gender, or to report b directly.

SummaryThree Takeaways

  • b carries the original units, β is in standard deviations; β = b·sx/sy.
  • Use β to compare predictors in different units; use b when the units mean something or the predictor is binary.
  • A proper report: direction + β/b + 95% CI + p + control variables + R², with "predicts," not "causes." Next lesson: {{L:3.4}}.
