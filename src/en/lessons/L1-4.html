① IntuitionHow much two variables "move together"

A plot full of dots

Chen, the social worker, puts the 800 residents on one plot: T1 loneliness total on the horizontal axis, T1 depression total on the vertical axis, one dot per person. She sees that the cloud of dots as a whole "tilts up to the right": people with higher loneliness scores tend to have higher depression scores too. But there are many exceptions: some people are very lonely but not depressed, others are not lonely but very depressed. How strong is this "tendency"? We need one number to sum it up.

This kind of plot is called a {{T:scatter plot|}}. Below is the real scatter plot of the course data (drawn by R/L1-4.R; to keep dots from sitting on top of each other, each one is moved by a small random amount, called "jitter"):

Covariance: think in quadrants

Draw a cross through the middle of the cloud: the vertical line at the mean of loneliness, the horizontal line at the mean of depression. Every dot now falls in one of four quadrants:

  • top right: both above average → deviations (+)(+) = positive;
  • bottom left: both below average → (−)(−) = positive;
  • top left and bottom right: one high, one low → negative.

Add up everyone's "product of deviations" and average it, and you have the {{T:covariance|}}. If most dots are in the top-right and bottom-left quadrants, the covariance is positive; if most are top-left and bottom-right, it is negative; if the four quadrants are about even, it is close to 0.

In the simulation above, tick "Show z-product quadrants" and drag ρ (read "rho"; it stands for the population correlation): the closer ρ is to 1, the more the dots gather in the green quadrants and the more they look like a straight line; at ρ = 0 the cloud is a round blob; when ρ is negative the dots gather in the red quadrants. Then set the sample size to 10 and click "Resample" a few times: in a small sample, the r you compute jumps around a lot around the population ρ. That is the topic of the next lesson.

From covariance to the correlation coefficient

Covariance has a drawback: its size depends on the scale. Whether loneliness is scored 4–16 or 0–100 changes the covariance many times over. You already know the fix: first turn both variables into z-scores, then compute the covariance. That is the {{T:correlation coefficient|}}, and its most common version is {{T:Pearson's r|}}. It always lies between −1 and 1:

rMeaning
+1Perfect positive correlation: all dots lie exactly on a rising straight line
0No linear relation (there may be a curved one)
−1Perfect negative correlation: all dots lie exactly on a falling straight line

The sign gives the direction, the absolute value gives the strength: r = −0.6 is stronger than r = 0.3. Psychologists often quote Cohen's rough guide: |r| ≈ 0.1 small, 0.3 medium, 0.5 large. It is only a guide, though; what counts as "large" differs between fields.

Other correlations: when the data are ordinal

  • {{T:Spearman's rank correlation|}}: replace each variable's values with ranks (1st, 2nd, ...) and compute the Pearson correlation of the ranks. English-language papers often write it as Spearman's ρ; this course uses ρ only for the population correlation, so to avoid confusion we always write the Spearman correlation as \(r_s\). It only cares whether "the orderings agree," so it is more robust to skew and extreme values.
  • {{T:polychoric correlation|}}: assumes that behind each Likert item there is a continuous, normally distributed "underlying tendency" that has been cut into a few categories; it estimates the correlation between these two underlying continuous variables. It is often used when items have few categories (such as 0–3) and are clearly skewed. qgraph::cor_auto(), widely used in network analysis, detects this automatically: variables with few categories (by default ≤ 7) are treated as ordinal, and it computes polychoric correlations.

In the course data, for "Depressed mood" and "Sleep problems" (T1): Pearson r = 0.40, Spearman \(r_s\) = 0.39, polychoric correlation = 0.48. The polychoric correlation is usually somewhat larger than the Pearson one, because the coarse 4 categories and the floor effect "push down" the Pearson correlation.

What affects the size of r? Restriction of range

A correlation depends not only on how tightly two variables are related, but also on how much the variables vary in the sample. If you only study people with "very high social support," almost everyone has the same social support, and its correlations with other variables are pushed down. In the course data, the correlation between social support and the loneliness total is −0.42 across all 800 people; among only the 185 people with social support ≥ 4, it shrinks to −0.22. This is called {{T:restriction of range|}}. Social work studies often sample only "service users" (whose problems are already concentrated in the higher range); the correlations found there may be weaker than in the general population, but that does not necessarily mean the relationship itself is weaker.

The correlation matrix: raw material for network analysis

With p variables, every pair has a correlation; arranged in a p × p table, they form a {{T:correlation matrix|}}. The diagonal is all 1s (each variable with itself), and the table is symmetric (the correlation of A with B = the correlation of B with A). With 9 items there are 9 × 8 / 2 = 36 different correlations. In Module 6 you will see that a cross-sectional network is "processed" almost entirely from this table; CLPN instead runs regressions on the relations between T1 and T2. The raw material of both kinds of network is a correlation (covariance) matrix of this kind.

Correlation ≠ causation: confounders

The classic example

In summer, ice cream sales and the number of drownings are strongly positively correlated. Does ice cream cause drowning? Of course not: hot weather makes people both buy ice cream and go swimming. A third variable that affects both like this is called a {{T:confounder|}}.

An example from social work: "number of community activities attended" is negatively correlated with "depression score." There are at least these possible explanations: (1) taking part in activities lowers depression; (2) reverse causation: depressed people don't want to go out to activities in the first place; (3) confounding: people in good physical health are both able to attend activities and less likely to be depressed; (4) selection bias: only people who were doing relatively well answered the questionnaire. One correlation coefficient cannot tell these four explanations apart.

② Essential MathCovariance and r

$$\operatorname{cov}(X,Y) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{n-1} \qquad r = \frac{\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)}{s_X\, s_Y} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n} z_{x_i}\, z_{y_i}$$
\((x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)\)
The product of person \(i\)'s two deviations: positive if they have the same sign, negative if not (the quadrant picture).
\(n-1\)
As with the variance, divide by \(n-1\).
\(s_X, s_Y\)
The standard deviation of each variable; dividing by them = removing the scale.
\(z_{x_i} z_{y_i}\)
The product of the two z-scores; r is the "average" of these products (dividing by \(n-1\)).

A quick check: what is the covariance of a variable with itself? \(\operatorname{cov}(X,X) = \sum (x_i-\bar x)^2/(n-1)\), which is the variance. So \(r(X,X) = s_X^2/(s_X s_X) = 1\).

A small example by hand

3 people: loneliness \(x\) = 2, 4, 6; depression \(y\) = 1, 3, 2.

  1. \(\bar x = 4,\ \bar y = 2\). Deviations: \(x\): −2, 0, 2; \(y\): −1, 1, 0.
  2. Products: 2, 0, 0, sum = 2; \(\operatorname{cov} = 2/2 = 1\).
  3. \(s_X = \sqrt{8/2}=2,\ s_Y=\sqrt{2/2}=1\); \(r = 1/(2\times1) = 0.5\).

Missing values

If the data contain NA, cor() returns NA by default. You can add use = "pairwise.complete.obs" (each pair of variables uses the cases that have both) or use = "complete.obs" (only cases with no missing values on any variable). {{L:2.5}} and {{L:4.3}} discuss the difference in detail.

Worked example: covariance and r by hand

5 people with loneliness x = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and depression y = 2, 4, 5, 4, 5.

  1. Means: x̄ = 3, ȳ = 4.
  2. Deviations: x: −2, −1, 0, 1, 2; y: −2, 0, 1, 0, 1.
  3. Products of deviations: 4, 0, 0, 0, 2, sum = 6, covariance = 6 ÷ 4 = 1.5.
  4. Standard deviations: the sum of squared deviations is 10 for x, SD = √(10/4) ≈ 1.581; 6 for y, SD = √(6/4) ≈ 1.225.
  5. r = 1.5 ÷ (1.581 × 1.225) ≈ 0.775.

You can check it in R: cor(c(1,2,3,4,5), c(2,4,5,4,5)). Notice that the dots with positive products (persons 1 and 5: both below, or both above, the means) push r up, while dots with a product of 0 (one variable exactly at its mean) contribute no information.

Common mistakes
  • Ignoring outliers. In a small sample, a single extreme case can move r from 0.1 to 0.4. Draw a scatter plot, or compute the Spearman correlation as a comparison.
  • Labeling r "strong" or "weak" without context. r = 0.3 is very common in psychosocial research and not small: r² = 0.09, so about 9% of the variance is "shared."

SummaryThree things to take away

  • Covariance = the average product of deviations; correlation r = the standardized covariance, from −1 to 1; the sign gives the direction, the absolute value the strength.
  • For ordinal, skewed items, consider the Spearman or polychoric correlation; the correlation matrix is the raw material of network analysis.
  • Correlation ≠ causation: reverse causation, confounding, selection bias, and other explanations remain. Next lesson: {{L:1.5}}.
