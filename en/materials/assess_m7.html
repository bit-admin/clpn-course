M7 module quiz

Take this after Lessons 7.1–7.5 (Week 13 of the standard pace). 10 auto-graded questions + 1 short-answer question, about 25 minutes. At the end of the page are 4 optional advanced questions; do them after Lessons 7.6 and 7.7. They do not count toward this module's score.

Auto-graded questions

The course data have 9 nodes (4 loneliness items, 5 depression items). How many regression equations do you fit to estimate a T1 → T2 CLPN?

A CLPN is estimated "nodewise": each T2 node gets its own regression equation.
9 T2 nodes, each predicted from all 9 T1 nodes: 9 regressions in total. See 7.1.

In the same 9-node CLPN, how many cross-lagged edges (not counting autoregressive edges) does the weights matrix B contain?

B is a 9 × 9 matrix; the autoregressive edges are on the diagonal.
9 × 9 = 81 cells, minus the 9 autoregressive edges on the diagonal, leaves 72 cross-lagged edges. See 7.1.

The course convention is that B[k, j] means "k at T1 → j at T2." If B["lone2", "dep3"] = 0.12, which reading is correct?

  • Correct.
  • The direction is reversed: rows are the T1 predictors, columns are the T2 outcomes.
  • A CLPN edge is a directed regression coefficient across time.
  • It is neither causal nor a percentage.
For what a CLPN edge means, see 7.1; for wording an interpretation, see Lesson 7.3.

What is the main difference between a CLPN and a CLPM?

  • A CLPN needs panel data with at least two waves.
  • Correct.
  • Both give only predictive relationships over time.
  • A standard CLPN, like a CLPM, does not separate them (see 7.5).
For how the CLPN relates to the CLPM and to cross-sectional networks, see 7.1.

Why do you standardize each item (z-scores) before running nodewise LASSO?

  • Standardizing does not change the shape of a distribution.
  • Correct.
  • Standardizing does nothing about missing data.
  • You still need to choose λ.
For why to standardize, see 7.2 and 3.5.

When you estimate a CLPN with cv.glmnet() and switch the λ rule from lambda.min to lambda.1se, what usually happens to the network?

  • The opposite direction.
  • Correct. It is more conservative, with fewer false positives, but may also miss weak true edges.
  • The two rules choose different λ values.
  • Not necessarily, and that is not the main difference between the two.
For the trade-off between lambda.min and lambda.1se, see 7.2. Whichever you use, state it in your analysis plan in advance.

The cross-validation in cv.glmnet() splits the data into folds at random. So that others (and your future self) can reproduce exactly the same CLPN, which R function must you call before running it? (Function name)

Its name contains "seed."
Write a line such as set.seed(2026) before running, and report the seed in your Methods. See 7.2.

In a CLPN weights matrix, the nonzero coefficients in row lone4 (lone4 at T1 predicting each node at T2) are: →lone4 0.40 (autoregressive), →dep1 0.10, →dep2 0.05, →dep4 −0.02; all others are 0. What is lone4's out-expected influence (out-EI, counting only cross-lagged edges)?

out-EI adds the coefficients of the cross-lagged edges in that row as they are (keeping the signs), without the autoregressive edge on the diagonal.
0.10 + 0.05 − 0.02 = 0.13. The autoregressive 0.40 is not counted. See 7.4.

"Depressed mood (dep1)" has the highest in-expected influence (in-EI) of the 9 nodes. Which statement is most appropriate?

  • That would be over-reading out-EI, and it is still causal language.
  • Correct.
  • Observational data cannot support an intervention conclusion like this.
  • It has nothing to do with measurement precision.
For what "predictive" and "predictable" nodes mean and their limits, see 7.4; for stability testing, see Lesson 8.3.

How does the course recommend adding age and gender as covariates in a CLPN?

  • Covariates are usually not nodes.
  • Correct.
  • The items still need to be standardized.
  • Binary variables such as gender work too.
For how to add covariates and why, see 7.5.

Optional advanced questions (7.6 / 7.7)

Before you use CLPNs to compare the edges of the T1→T2 and T2→T3 networks, which level of measurement invariance do you most need to establish first?

  • Configural invariance only says "the structure is the same"; it does not guarantee that each item is related to the latent trait equally strongly.
  • Correct. Scalar (intercept) invariance is what you need to compare means.
  • Scalar invariance is the prerequisite for comparing means; comparing edges first requires equal loadings.
  • If an item's meaning drifts, a change in the edges may just be a change in measurement.
For what loading invariance and intercept invariance are each a prerequisite for, see 7.6.

When you use simulation to estimate "how many people are needed to detect the target edges," which of the following is not an assumption the estimate depends on?

  • This is the most important assumption.
  • lambda.min and lambda.1se give different results.
  • The more nodes, the more people you need.
  • Correct. This has nothing to do with sample size planning.
For sample size simulation and the assumptions it depends on, see 7.6.

You ran 999 permutations, and in 24 of them the shuffled M was ≥ the observed M. Using the course formula, what is the permutation p-value?

p = (number of times ≥ the observed value + 1) / (number of permutations + 1).
(24 + 1) / (999 + 1) = 0.025. See 7.7.

Comparing the CLPNs of 150 urban and 150 rural older adults, the M test gives p = .38. What is the most appropriate conclusion?

  • Not significant ≠ the same.
  • Correct.
  • The number of permutations does not fix a lack of power.
  • Changing the seed to pick a result is p-hacking.
For "not significant ≠ the same" and sample size in group comparisons, see 7.7.

Short-answer questions

Short answer 1

In a CLPN (N = 600, lambda.min), the strongest cross-lagged edge is "Feeling left out" (lone2) at T1 → "Loss of interest" (dep2) at T2, B = 0.14. Write a 2–3 sentence interpretation for the Results section of a paper: read the direction correctly, give the number, avoid causal language, and add one limitation.

Reference answer & rubric

Example: "Controlling for all 9 items at T1, participants who scored higher on 'Feeling left out' at T1 tended to score higher on 'Loss of interest' at T2 (B = 0.14); this was the strongest cross-lagged edge in the network. This result reflects a predictive relationship over time and cannot be interpreted as a causal effect; a standard CLPN also does not separate within-person from between-person effects, so whether this edge represents a change process within individuals needs further study (together with a bootstrap check of its stability)."

LevelCriteria
3Correct direction (lone2 at T1 → dep2 at T2, controlling for the other T1 nodes), gives B = 0.14, no causal language, and adds a reasonable limitation (causality / within- vs between-person / stability)
2Direction and number correct, but the limitation is missing or vague, or there is one mildly causal phrase
1Direction reversed, or clearly causal language
0No answer, or wrong
