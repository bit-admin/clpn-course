M3 module quiz

Take this after Lessons 3.1–3.5 (Week 8 of the standard pace, together with the M4 module quiz). 10 auto-graded questions + 1 short-answer question, about 20 minutes. You may use a calculator.

Auto-graded questions

Predicting "loneliness score" (y) from "social activities per week" (x) gives the regression equation ŷ = 3.2 − 0.15x. What does the slope −0.15 mean?

  • The slope is not a percentage, and you cannot say social activity "reduces" loneliness from it.
  • Correct.
  • The predicted value at x = 0 is the intercept, 3.2.
  • A slope and a correlation are generally not equal unless both variables are standardized.
Slope = how much ŷ changes on average for each 1-unit increase in x; see 3.1.

Using the same equation, ŷ = 3.2 − 0.15x, what is the predicted loneliness score of an older adult who takes part in 4 social activities a week?

Substitute x = 4 into the equation.
3.2 − 0.15 × 4 = 3.2 − 0.6 = 2.6; see 3.1.

What condition does the regression line chosen by least squares satisfy?

  • No.
  • Correct.
  • No.
  • No.
The smallest sum of squared residuals is what "least squares" means; see 3.1.

You predict depression from "social support" and "age" together, and the coefficient for social support is −0.40. How should you read this coefficient?

  • The key is the condition "holding age constant."
  • Correct; this is what "controlling for age" means.
  • That is how you describe R², not a coefficient.
  • The question does not give the coefficient for age.
A partial regression coefficient means "with the other variables held constant"; see 3.2.

Predicting depression from "loneliness" alone gives a coefficient of 0.50. After you add "social support," the loneliness coefficient drops to 0.30. What is the most likely explanation?

  • Coefficients often change; it is not an error.
  • Correct.
  • These are the same cross-sectional data; time plays no role.
  • No.
Coefficients change when you control for other variables because the predictors overlap; see 3.2.

A researcher puts the "UCLA Loneliness total score" and "the total score of another loneliness scale" into the same regression. They correlate at 0.92. What problem is most likely?

  • It won't.
  • Correct.
  • Quite the opposite: often neither is.
  • Highly correlated predictors do cause problems.
For multicollinearity and its effects, see 3.2. In network analysis, items with heavily overlapping content cause the same problem.

In predicting depression, the unstandardized coefficient of "monthly income (in yuan, the Chinese currency unit)" is b = −0.0004 and that of "social support (1–5 points)" is b = −0.35. Can you conclude from this that social support matters far more than income?

  • The units differ, so the b values cannot be compared directly.
  • Correct.
  • The size of b cannot tell you that.
  • The sign is not the reason.
b carries the units of the variables; β is the standardized coefficient and is suited to comparing relative size; see 3.3.

A logistic regression predicts "whether an adolescent drops out of school." The odds ratio for "classes skipped per week" is OR = 1.5. How should you read it?

  • An OR is a multiple of the odds, not percentage points of probability.
  • Correct.
  • No.
  • Only OR = 1 means no relationship.
For how to read odds and odds ratios (OR), see 3.4.

What is the key difference between LASSO and ordinary regression?

  • It can use many.
  • Correct. A CLPN uses this to decide which edges to keep.
  • LASSO coefficients are usually smaller (shrunk).
  • Of course it does.
For the LASSO penalty and how it sets coefficients to 0, see 3.5.

After cross-validation with cv.glmnet, you can choose lambda.min or lambda.1se. Compared with lambda.min, what kind of model does lambda.1se give?

  • The opposite direction.
  • Correct.
  • They usually differ.
  • Usually not all are 0.
lambda.1se is the largest λ whose cross-validated error is within 1 standard error of the minimum, so its model is sparser; see 3.5.

Short-answer questions

Short answer 1

A colleague built a regression model from 40 clients' data with 30 predictors. It predicts "almost perfectly" for those 40 people, and the colleague plans to use it to screen new clients. In 3–4 sentences, explain the likely problem to them and give one concrete recommendation.

Reference answer & rubric

With only 40 people and 30 predictors, the model has probably "memorized" chance features (noise) of these 40 people. This is called overfitting: predicting the old data well does not mean predicting new clients accurately. Recommendation: test the predictions with cross-validation or a new batch of data; use fewer predictors, or use a regularization method such as LASSO to make the model simpler.

LevelCriteria
3Names overfitting (memorizing noise), identifies few people and many predictors as the cause, and gives a concrete recommendation such as cross-validation, testing on a new sample, or regularization
2Names overfitting, but the cause or the recommendation is incomplete
1Only says the model is unreliable, without explaining why or giving an actionable recommendation
0No answer, or says the model can be used as it is
