M4 module quiz

Take this after Lessons 4.1–4.3 (Week 8 of the standard pace, together with the M3 module quiz). 8 auto-graded questions + 1 short-answer question, about 15 minutes.

Auto-graded questions

Each year for 5 years, a city surveys loneliness in a new random sample of 1000 residents. What kind of design is this?

  • A panel design measures the same people again and again.
  • Correct. Each year has different people, so you can see population trends but not how the same person changes.
  • A plain cross-sectional design surveys only once.
  • Nobody is randomly assigned to an intervention.
For the difference between cross-sectional, repeated cross-sectional and panel designs, see 4.1. A CLPN needs panel data.

A researcher wants to know whether "poor sleep leads to low mood the next day," but measures only once a year. What is the main problem?

  • The opposite direction.
  • Correct.
  • The interval does affect which effects you can see.
  • A cross-sectional design is even less able to say what comes "after."
The interval between waves (the lag) has to match the process you want to study; see 4.1 (Lesson 5.4 goes deeper).

"People who are lonelier over the long run are also more depressed over the long run" and "during the periods when a person is lonelier than usual for them, they are also more depressed than usual for them." At which levels are these two relationships?

  • Correct.
  • The other way round.
  • The second compares a person with their own usual level, so it is within-person.
  • They can be completely different; this is one of the core difficulties of longitudinal research.
For between-person vs within-person, see 4.1; the RI-CLPM in Lesson 5.3 separates the two.

A loneliness scale was given on paper in Year 1 and on mobile phones in Year 3, and the Year 3 mean is clearly higher. Before you conclude that "people have become lonelier," what do you need to check first?

  • Sample size cannot answer this.
  • Correct. A change in how people respond can change how they understand the items or where they draw the line when answering.
  • Irrelevant.
  • Irrelevant.
For what measurement invariance means, see 4.1; the optional advanced Lesson 7.6 shows you how to test it in R.

The course data have 800 people and 3 waves; in wide format each person is one row. After you convert it to long format (one row per person per wave), how many rows are there?

In long format, each person takes one row for every wave.
800 × 3 = 2400 rows. For wide vs long format, see 4.2.

When you prepare the "T1 item matrix" and the "T2 item matrix" for a CLPN, what is the key requirement?

  • With different numbers of rows, the rows cannot be matched.
  • Correct. Otherwise you would be using person A's T1 to predict person B's T2.
  • Shuffling destroys the matching of cases.
  • Both matrices should contain the same items.
Matching cases one to one is the bare minimum of CLPN data preparation; see 4.2.

In a panel survey, people with higher depression scores at Wave 1 are more likely to drop out by Wave 2; but once you know their Wave 1 depression score, whether they drop out is unrelated to how depressed they would have been at Wave 2. What kind of missingness is this?

  • Dropout is related to Wave 1 depression, so it is not completely random.
  • Correct. In this case FIML or multiple imputation can use the Wave 1 information.
  • MNAR means missingness depends on the unobserved value itself, which is not what the question describes.
  • Dropout is missing data.
For the difference between MCAR, MAR and MNAR, see 4.3.

In three-wave data, you delete a person's whole row if any wave has a missing value (listwise deletion). What is the main risk?

  • Deleting data only makes them faster.
  • Correct.
  • Quite the opposite.
  • There is.
For the pros and cons of listwise deletion, pairwise deletion, FIML and multiple imputation, see 4.3.

Short-answer questions

Short answer 1

In a three-wave panel study of adolescents, only 60% of participants remain at Wave 3. In 3–4 sentences: (1) Why does attrition threaten the study's conclusions? (2) What analysis would you do to check the effect of attrition?

Reference answer & rubric

(1) If the people who stay differ from those who drop out (for example, the dropouts were lonelier at Wave 1 or came from families in greater difficulty), the Wave 3 results represent only "those who stayed," and both the mean levels and the relationships between variables may be biased. (2) Do an attrition analysis: compare stayers and dropouts on the main Wave 1 variables (loneliness, depression, gender, age, etc.), e.g. with t-tests, chi-square tests, or a logistic regression predicting dropout; report the attrition rate, handle the missing data with a method such as FIML or multiple imputation, and run a sensitivity analysis if needed.

LevelCriteria
3Explains clearly that stayers and dropouts differ, so the results are biased, and gives a concrete attrition analysis (comparing Wave 1 variables or predicting dropout with logistic regression) and a way to handle the missing data
2Gives either the threat or the analysis; the other is incomplete
1Only says "the sample got smaller," without systematic bias or a concrete analysis
0No answer, or wrong
