TermOne-Sentence DefinitionIntroduced In

study logNotes on what you studied each time, your questions and what you learned; the course provides a template.0.1

M1 Statistics Foundations

TermOne-Sentence DefinitionIntroduced In
variableA characteristic whose value differs between people (or over time), such as age or a loneliness score.1.1
level of measurementHow much information the numbers of a variable carry: nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio.1.1
nominal / ordinalNominal values only distinguish categories (gender); ordinal values also have an order, but the steps need not be equal (Likert ratings).1.1
interval / ratioNumbers with equal steps; ratio scales also have a true zero (income, hours of sleep).1.1
Likert scaleAn item format answered with ordered options such as "never" to "often".1.1
constructA psychological concept that cannot be observed directly and is measured with several items, such as loneliness or depression.1.1
itemA single question of a scale; the nodes of a CLPN are usually items.1.1
reliabilityHow consistent and stable a measurement is, e.g. test-retest reliability or internal consistency (α).1.1
validityWhether a measure really measures what it is meant to measure.1.1
distributionThe pattern of how often each value of a variable occurs, usually shown as a histogram.1.2
meanThe sum of all values divided by their number: the average.1.2
medianThe middle value after sorting; not affected by extreme values.1.2
varianceThe average squared deviation from the mean (divided by n − 1 for a sample variance); a measure of spread.1.2
standard deviation, SDThe square root of the variance, in the same units as the raw scores.1.2
normal distributionA symmetric, bell-shaped distribution that many statistical methods use as a reference.1.2
skewnessAn asymmetric distribution; right skew means a long tail on the right (e.g. when most people have very low depression scores).1.2
ceiling / floor effectMany people bunch up at the highest or lowest score of a scale, so the scale distinguishes less well between them.1.2
standardizationSubtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation, so the variable has mean 0 and SD 1.1.3
z-scoreA standardized value: how many standard deviations a score lies from the mean.1.3
scale / metricThe units and range of a measurement, such as 0–3 or 0–100.1.3
scatter plotA plot with one variable on each axis and one point per person, used to look at a relationship.1.4
covarianceThe average product of two variables' deviations from their means; positive when they move together.1.4
correlation coefficientA standardized covariance between −1 and 1 that measures the strength and direction of a straight-line relationship.1.4
Pearson's rThe most common correlation coefficient, suited to continuous, roughly normal variables.1.4
Spearman's ρA correlation based on ranks; more robust to outliers and to nonlinear monotonic relationships.1.4
polychoric correlationA method that estimates the correlation of ordinal items by assuming continuous normal variables behind them.1.4
correlation matrixA square table of all pairwise correlations among several variables, with 1s on the diagonal.1.4
confounderA variable related to both the predictor and the outcome that distorts the relationship between them.1.4
populationAll the units a study wants to draw conclusions about.1.5
sampleThe part of the population that was actually studied.1.5
parameterA number describing the population (usually unknown), such as the population mean μ.1.5
statisticA number computed from the sample to estimate a parameter, such as the sample mean.1.5
sampling distributionThe distribution of a statistic's values over repeated samples.1.5
standard error, SEThe standard deviation of a statistic's sampling distribution; a measure of how precise an estimate is.1.5
confidence interval, CIAn interval built by a procedure that, over repeated samples, covers the true value a given share of the time (e.g. 95%).1.5
sample sizeThe number of cases in the sample, N.1.5
null hypothesis, H₀The hypothesis, usually of "no effect / no difference", that a test starts from.1.6
alternative hypothesisThe hypothesis opposed to the null, usually the one the researcher hopes to support.1.6
p-valueThe probability of a result as extreme as the one observed, or more extreme, if the null hypothesis were true.1.6
significance level, αThe cut-off set in advance for a decision, usually α = .05.1.6
Type I / Type II errorType I: rejecting a true null hypothesis (false positive); Type II: not rejecting a false null hypothesis (false negative).1.6
statistical powerThe probability that a test detects an effect that really exists (1 − Type II error rate).1.6
effect sizeA measure of the size of an effect that does not depend on sample size, such as Cohen's d, r or β.1.6
multiple comparisonsThe problem that false positives pile up when you run many tests.1.6

M3 Regression

TermOne-Sentence DefinitionIntroduced In
regressionA model that predicts one variable from one or more others.3.1
predictorA variable on the right-hand side of a regression equation, used to predict.3.1
outcomeThe variable on the left-hand side of a regression equation, the one being predicted.3.1
interceptThe predicted outcome when all predictors are 0.3.1
slope / coefficientThe average change in the predicted outcome when a predictor increases by 1 unit.3.1
residualObserved value minus predicted value.3.1
ordinary least squares, OLSAn estimation method that chooses the coefficients minimizing the sum of squared residuals.3.1
R²R², the proportion of the outcome's variance explained by the model.3.1
multiple regressionA regression with several predictors at once.3.2
partial regression coefficientThe coefficient of one predictor in a multiple regression, with the other predictors held constant.3.2
covariate / control variableA variable put into a model to remove its influence.3.2
multicollinearityPredictors that are highly correlated with each other, which makes coefficients unstable and standard errors large.3.2
interactionThe effect of one variable depends on the value of another.3.2
standardized coefficient, ββ, the coefficient after all variables are standardized; its unit is the standard deviation.3.3
unstandardized coefficient, bb, the coefficient in the original units.3.3
APA styleThe writing and reporting style of the American Psychological Association, common in psychology and social work.3.3
logistic regressionThe regression model used when the outcome is binary (yes / no).3.4
probabilityHow likely an event is, between 0 and 1.3.4
oddsOdds: the probability that something happens divided by the probability that it doesn't.3.4
log-odds / logitLogit: the natural log of the odds; logistic regression is linear on this scale.3.4
odds ratio, OROR (odds ratio): the factor by which the odds change when a predictor increases by 1 unit.3.4
generalized linear model, GLMA family of models that extends linear regression to binary, count and other outcomes.3.4
overfittingA model also "learns" the random noise in the sample and does worse on new data.3.5
bias–variance trade-offSimpler models have more bias but are stable; more complex models have less bias but vary more.3.5
regularizationPenalizing the size of the coefficients during estimation to reduce overfitting.3.5
ridge regressionA regularization method that penalizes the sum of squared coefficients; coefficients shrink but never become 0.3.5
LASSOA regularization method that penalizes the sum of absolute coefficients; it can shrink small coefficients to exactly 0 and so selects variables.3.5
elastic netA mix of ridge regression and LASSO.3.5
tuning parameter, lambdaThe number that sets the strength of the penalty; the larger λ, the simpler the model.3.5
cross-validation, CVSplitting the data into several parts, testing on each part in turn and training on the rest, to assess predictive performance.3.5
sparsityMost coefficients are 0 and only a few are nonzero.3.5

M6 Network Psychometrics

TermOne-Sentence DefinitionIntroduced In
nodeOne variable in a network (a circle).6.1
edgeA statistical association between two nodes (a line).6.1
edge weightThe size and sign of an edge.6.1
directed / undirectedA directed edge has an arrow (A → B); an undirected edge has no direction.6.1
adjacency matrixA square matrix recording whether there is an edge (and its weight) between each pair of nodes.6.1
weights matrixAn adjacency matrix filled with the edge weights.6.1
densityThe number of edges present as a proportion of all possible edges.6.1
community / clusterA group of nodes that are closely connected among themselves, such as the items of one scale.6.1
partial correlationThe correlation between two variables after controlling for the other variables.6.2
conditional independenceTwo variables are no longer correlated after controlling for the others; in a GGM this means "no edge".6.2
Gaussian graphical model (GGM)GGM, an undirected network model whose edges are partial correlations.6.2
Markov random fieldA family of network models that use undirected edges for conditional dependence.6.2
precision matrixThe inverse of the covariance matrix; it can be converted into partial correlations.6.2
indirect associationAn association that is not direct but runs through a mediator (A → B → C) or a common cause (A ← B → C); partial correlations cannot tell the two apart.6.2
graphical lasso (glasso)glasso: putting a LASSO penalty on the precision matrix to get a sparse network.6.3
extended Bayesian information criterion (EBIC)EBIC, a model selection criterion that adds a heavier penalty on the number of edges than BIC.6.3
hyperparameter gamma (γ)The EBIC parameter that controls how sparse the network is; usually 0.5.6.3
sparse networkA network in which most possible edges are 0.6.3
false positive edgeAn edge that is estimated but does not actually exist.6.3
unregularized estimation (e.g., ggmModSelect)Estimating a network without a penalty, e.g. by stepwise selection.6.3
estimateNetwork()bootnet's estimateNetwork(), which estimates a network in one step.6.4
layout (e.g., spring / Fruchterman–Reingold)How the nodes are placed in a plot, e.g. a spring layout.6.4
grouping / node coloring (groups)Coloring nodes by scale or construct (the groups argument).6.4
maximum edge weight (maximum)maximum: fixes the weight that the thickest edge stands for, so that several plots can be compared.6.4
legendThe part of a figure that explains node colors or labels.6.4
centralityA family of indices of how "important" a node is in a network.6.5
strengthThe sum of the absolute weights of all of a node's edges.6.5
expected influence (EI)EI: the sum of the (signed) weights of all of a node's edges.6.5
betweenness centralityHow often a node lies on the shortest paths between other nodes; unstable in psychological networks, so use with caution.6.5
closeness centralityThe inverse of a node's average distance to all other nodes; use with caution.6.5
bridge centralityHow strongly a node is connected to nodes of other communities.6.5
bridge expected influenceThe sum of the signed weights of a node's edges to nodes of other communities.6.5
comorbidityTwo problems occurring together, such as loneliness and depression.6.5

M7 Cross-Lagged Panel Networks

TermOne-Sentence DefinitionIntroduced In
cross-lagged panel network (CLPN)CLPN, a directed network estimated at the item level, with T1 → T2 autoregressive and cross-lagged edges.7.1
directed edgeAn edge with an arrow, showing the direction of prediction over time.7.1
autoregressive edgeIn a CLPN, the edge from a node at T1 to the same node at T2.7.1
cross-lagged edgeIn a CLPN, the edge from a node at T1 to a different node at T2.7.1
nodewise regressionRunning a separate regression for each outcome node and combining the results into a network.7.1
time point t → t+1The period from one wave to the next.7.1
cross-construct edgeAn edge between different constructs (e.g. loneliness item → depression item).7.1
for loopA for loop, which runs the same code repeatedly.7.2
random seedset.seed(), which makes random processes reproducible.7.2
coefficient matrixThe p × p matrix B of a CLPN: rows = T1 predictors, columns = T2 outcomes.7.2
lambda.min / lambda.1seThe λ with the smallest cross-validation error / the largest λ whose error is within 1 standard error of that minimum.7.2
covariateA variable you control for but do not include as a network node, such as gender or age.7.2
analysis decision logA document recording each analysis choice and the reason for it.7.2
directed network plotA network plot that shows direction with arrows.7.3
self-loopAn edge from a node to itself; in a CLPN it stands for the autoregressive edge.7.3
display thresholdHiding edges below some value in a plot only; the model is not changed.7.3
figure captionThe text below a figure that explains what it shows and its conventions.7.3
edge listA table with one edge per row (from, to, weight).7.3
out-expected influence (out-EI)Out-EI: the sum of the cross-lagged edge weights from a node to the other nodes.7.4
in-expected influence (in-EI)In-EI: the sum of the cross-lagged edge weights from the other nodes into a node.7.4
out-strength / in-strengthThe sum of the absolute weights of a node's outgoing / incoming edges.7.4
bridge out-EIThe sum of the cross-lagged edge weights from a node to nodes of the other construct.7.4
driver node (caution advised)A label often given to nodes with high out-EI; it implies causation, so use it with caution.7.4
multi-wave CLPNA CLPN with three or more waves, estimated interval by interval or jointly.7.5
network replicationHow similar a network turns out in another interval or another sample.7.5
panel graphical VAR (panel GVAR)A multilevel network model (panel graphical VAR) that can separate between-person and within-person networks.7.5
psychonetricsAn R package that can estimate panel network models.7.5
penalty.factorglmnet's penalty.factor; setting it to 0 leaves that variable unpenalized.7.5
sample size planningWorking out in advance how large a sample you need to estimate a network stably.7.5
configural invarianceThe first level of measurement invariance: each wave or group measures the same factors with the same items (the same structure), but parameters may differ.7.6
metric / weak invarianceThe second level of measurement invariance: each item's factor loading is equal across waves or groups, so correlations and regression coefficients can be compared.7.6
scalar / strong invarianceThe third level of measurement invariance: loadings and item intercepts are both equal, so factor means can be compared (who is higher, whether it rose).7.6
partial invarianceInvariance in which most items' parameters are equal and a few items are freed; the freed items must be named in the paper.7.6
factor loadingHow much an item's score rises on average when the factor (the latent true level) rises by 1 unit; think of it as the item's "sensitivity".7.6
item interceptThe expected item score when the factor is 0 (at the reference level); think of it as the item's "zero point" or "starting point".7.6
multi-group CFAFitting the same factor model in two or more groups at once and adding equality constraints step by step to test measurement invariance.7.6
score test / Lagrange multiplier testA test of "how much would chi-square drop if this one constraint were freed", used to find the worst-fitting equality constraint (also called the Lagrange multiplier test; related to modification indices).7.6
ΔCFIThe CFI of the stricter model minus that of the looser model; a value no lower than −0.01 is usually taken to mean the constraints did not make fit clearly worse.7.6
Monte Carlo simulationGenerating and analyzing data many times under an assumed true model, and averaging over the runs to answer "how does the method perform under these conditions?".7.6
false positive rateThe proportion of edges that are essentially 0 in truth but estimated as nonzero (the rate of false positives).7.6
permutation testShuffling the group labels at random and recomputing the statistic many times gives its distribution when "the groups do not differ"; then you check whether the observed value is unusual.7.7
null distributionThe distribution of a statistic's possible values if the null hypothesis holds (e.g. no difference between groups); a permutation test gets it by shuffling.7.7
group labelThe variable in the data that says which group each person belongs to, such as gender or whether they received a service.7.7
network structure invariance test (M)Tests whether the largest difference in any single edge between two groups' networks (M) is beyond what random grouping produces.7.7
global strength invariance test (S)Tests whether the difference in the sum of absolute edge weights (S) between two groups' networks is beyond what random grouping produces.7.7
edge invariance testCompares the same edge in two groups' networks, one edge at a time; with many edges, correct for multiple comparisons or test only edges specified in advance.7.7
Holm correctionA correction for multiple comparisons: sort the p-values from smallest to largest and multiply each in turn by the number of tests remaining; slightly less strict than Bonferroni.7.7
pre-specified hypothesisA specific hypothesis written into the analysis plan before you look at the data (e.g. comparing only one edge), so you pay no multiple-comparison cost for edges you did not test.7.7
NetworkComparisonTestAn R package (NCT) for comparing two cross-sectional networks, with permutation tests for M, S and single edges; the course does not install it and only shows the code in Lesson 7.7.7.7

parallel computing (nCores)Using several CPU cores at once (nCores > 1); faster, but bootnet's parallel resampling is not controlled by set.seed, so results differ slightly each run (the course default is nCores = 1).8.2
case-dropping bootstrapDropping larger and larger proportions of cases to see whether the centrality ranking holds.8.3
correlation stability coefficient (CS-coefficient)CS-coefficient: the largest proportion of cases you can drop and re-estimate while, in at least 95% of subsamples, centrality still correlates ≥ 0.7 (ordinary Pearson) with centrality in the full sample.8.3
centrality stabilityHow far the centrality ranking stays the same when the sample changes.8.3
rank-order consistencyThe correlation between two rankings.8.3
