① IntuitionWhere this course will take you

An afternoon at a community social work station

Suppose you are on a placement at a neighborhood social work station, working on a mental health support program for local residents. Your supervisor shows you a panel survey: the same 800 adult residents (aged 18–85, mean age about 45) filled in a questionnaire every 6 months, three times in all. The questionnaire includes items such as "I lack companionship," "I feel left out," "I feel down," "I sleep badly," and "I feel worthless." Your supervisor asks: "Of all these problems, which one, early on, best predicts that the others will get worse later? Where should our limited resources go first?"

The question has three key features: the same people measured several times (time), each item rather than the total score, and who predicts whom (direction). The cross-lagged panel network (CLPN) taught in this course is one method that handles all three at once.

The whole course uses one dataset of exactly this kind: 800 adult community residents, aged 18–85 (mean about 45), three waves, 6 months apart. The data are simulated (see section ③ of this lesson) and contain no real personal information. Whenever later lessons mention "residents," they mean adults like these.

CLPN is a type of network analysis: it treats each item as a "node" and draws the statistical relations between items as "edges." It is also a longitudinal study method: it uses the situation at the first measurement (T1) to predict the situation at the second measurement (T2), so its edges have arrows.

The last module of the course (M10) is a capstone project: with this three-wave dataset (4 loneliness items + 5 depression items, N = 800) you carry out the whole workflow on your own, from data cleaning, CLPN estimation, plotting, centrality, and stability checks to writing up and presenting the results, and you hand in a report that reruns with one click.

Why not start with networks right away?

The house-building analogy

CLPN is the roof. A roof sits on walls, and walls stand on a foundation. Specifically:

  • Foundation = statistics basics (M1). A CLPN produces numbers: a coefficient of 0.12, an interval of [0.03, 0.20], and so on. Without the mean, variance, correlation, and standard error, you cannot read these numbers.
  • Tools = R (M2). All computation is done in R. You learn M1 statistics and M2 R side by side (alternating in weeks 2–5).
  • Walls = regression (M3). Technically, a CLPN is "one regression for each T2 item." Regression is its engine, and LASSO is its brakes. M3 comes after M1 and M2.
  • Building materials = longitudinal data (M4). How to arrange the data as "one table for T1, one table for T2, with the same person on the same row," and what to do about missing data.
  • The neighbors' houses = CLPM (M5) and cross-sectional networks (M6). CLPN combines these two methods: it borrows the idea of "cross-lagged" effects from the CLPM, and "nodes, edges, centrality, and regularization" from network psychometrics.
  • Roof = CLPN (M7), inspection = stability checks (M8), handover paperwork = reading, critique, and reporting (M9), moving in = capstone project (M10).

The module numbers give the order of study. You learn M1 statistics and M2 R side by side (alternating in weeks 2–5): as soon as you learn a statistical idea, you try it in R. M3 regression comes after both. The "Study pacing" table on the syllabus page suggests a 16-week schedule.

② Essential MathNotation used throughout the course

Every later lesson uses the notation below. Spend a few minutes on it now and formulas will be much easier to read later.

1. Subscripts: which person, which item, which wave

\(x_i\) is read "x sub i" and means "the value of \(x\) for person \(i\)." If 6 people have loneliness total scores of 8, 10, 9, 14, 7, 12, then \(x_1 = 8\) and \(x_4 = 14\). In longitudinal data we often add a time subscript: \(x_{i,t}\) is the score of person \(i\) at measurement \(t\); for example, \(x_{3,2}\) is person 3's score at T2.

2. The summation sign Σ

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i = x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_n$$
\(\sum\)
Capital Greek sigma; it means "add up everything that follows."
\(i=1\) to \(n\)
Add from person 1 to person \(n\); \(n\) is the sample size (number of people).
\(x_i\)
What gets added each time: the value of person \(i\).

For the 6 people above: \(\sum x_i = 8+10+9+14+7+12 = 60\). Divide the total by the number of people and you get the mean, \(\bar{x} = 60/6 = 10\) (\(\bar{x}\) is read "x-bar" and denotes the mean; more in 1.2 Distributions and Descriptive Statistics: Mean, Variance, and Standard Deviation).

3. Hats and Greek letters

Statistics uses two sets of letters. Greek letters (\(\mu, \sigma, \beta, \rho\)) usually stand for "true values" we want to know but cannot see; letters with a hat (\(\hat{\beta}\), read "beta-hat") stand for values estimated from data. For example, \(\hat{y}\) is the \(y\) "predicted" by a regression. Every edge in a CLPN is a \(\hat{\beta}\).

4. A preview: the CLPN equation

In Lesson 7.1 you will see the equation below. You don't need to understand it now; just notice that it is built from the parts above:

$$X_{j,\,t+1} = \sum_{k=1}^{p} \beta_{kj}\, X_{k,\,t} + \varepsilon_j$$

Read it as: "The score on item \(j\) at the next measurement (\(t+1\)) equals the scores on all \(p\) items at this measurement (\(t\)), each multiplied by a coefficient \(\beta_{kj}\) and added up, plus an error \(\varepsilon_j\) that cannot be predicted (Greek epsilon)." The coefficient \(\beta_{kj}\) is how thick the arrow from item \(k\) to item \(j\) is.

The course data are simulated: a program generated them from a known "true network" (see data/simulate_data.R). The benefit is that once you have learned the method, you can compare the network you estimate with the known answer and check whether the method "recovers" the structure that was built in. In real research you cannot do this.

The code below is taken from R/_setup.R. It is a simplified version of the core of CLPN estimation, which you should be able to read fully and write yourself in 16 weeks. For now, just read the comments to get a feel for the destination:

Preview: the core loop of a CLPN (covered in Lesson 7.2; no need to understand it yet)
X <- scale(as.matrix(d1))   # The 9 items at T1, standardized first (Lesson 1.3)
Y <- scale(as.matrix(d2))   # The 9 items at T2: same people, same order (Lesson 4.2)
B <- matrix(0, 9, 9)        # An empty 9 × 9 table to hold the coefficients
for (j in 1:9) {            # For each T2 item... (Lesson 2.2: loops)
  cvfit <- glmnet::cv.glmnet(X, Y[, j], alpha = 1)       # ...fit one LASSO regression (Lesson 3.5)
  B[, j] <- as.numeric(coef(cvfit, s = "lambda.min"))[-1] # Put the coefficients in column j
}
B                           # This 9 × 9 table is a directed network

Count them: this code uses standardization (1.3 Standardization and z-Scores), matrices and loops (2.2 R Basic Syntax: Vectors, Data Types, and Functions), aligning data across waves (4.2 Wide vs. Long Formats: Data Reshaping), and LASSO with cross-validation (3.5 Overfitting and Regularization: Ridge, LASSO, and Cross-Validation).

④ Concept CheckQuick quiz

What does the last module of the course (M10) ask you to produce?

  • Reading papers and writing review comments happen in M9, as preparation for the capstone; they are not the final product.
  • M10 asks you to carry out the whole workflow yourself: data preparation, estimation, plotting, centrality, stability, writing, and presenting.
  • The network plot is only one step; M10 also requires stability checks, a written results section, a discussion of limitations, and a report that reruns with one click.
  • A cross-sectional network (M6) has no time direction, so it cannot answer "who predicts later change in whom," which is exactly the question CLPN answers.
Knowing the destination helps you see why each lesson is there.

Why do statistics basics and R come before network analysis?

  • Every line in a network plot is a statistical estimate; just reading the plot requires you to understand coefficients, intervals, and stability.
  • The order follows what depends on what, not what is easier: without regression, you cannot read a single coefficient a CLPN produces.
  • Correct. CLPN = one LASSO regression for each T2 item, and regression rests on correlation and variance.
This is the house-building analogy: the roof (CLPN) sits on the walls (regression) and the foundation (statistics).

Which of these questions can only be answered by measuring the same people more than once?

  • One measurement (cross-sectional data) is enough to look at the correlation; it shows that the two occur together, not which comes first.
  • Correct. "Predicting later change" needs data on the same person at two time points.
  • One measurement is enough to compare two group means; no time order is involved.
  • This is a descriptive question; one survey is enough.
Whenever a question contains "later," "change," or "predict the next measurement," you usually need longitudinal (panel) data.

In the formula \(\sum_{i=1}^{4} x_i\), if \(x_1=2, x_2=3, x_3=1, x_4=4\), what is the result?

Σ means "add them all up": from the 1st to the 4th.
2 + 3 + 1 + 4 = 10.

What does the symbol \(\hat{\beta}\) (beta-hat) usually stand for?

  • The true value is \(\beta\) without a hat.
  • Correct. Hat = "estimate."
  • The hat only says "estimated from a sample"; it has nothing to do with standardization. A standardized coefficient can have a hat or not.
The basic situation in statistics: we want to know the true \(\beta\), but we can only compute \(\hat\beta\) from a sample and judge how uncertain it is (Lesson 1.5).

Which statement about the course's simulated data is correct?

  • A program generated them from a known network (see data/simulate_data.R); they contain no real personal information.
  • Correct. This is the main benefit of using simulated data.
  • Sample estimates still have sampling error, and LASSO also shrinks small edges to 0. Lesson 7.2 will also show that what a CLPN targets in the population is not the same as the within-person network used to generate the data.
In the capstone project you will compare your estimated CLPN with the population target in data/true_B_population.csv.

Exercise 0.1-A: Where you are starting from (about 1 page)

For each of statistics, programming, and research methods, write down: (1) what you already know; (2) what worries you most; (3) where you hope to be after 16 weeks.

Writing tips and an example (write yours first, then open)

There is no "right answer"; what matters is being specific. Compare:

  • Not specific: "I'm not very good at statistics."
  • Specific: "I know means and percentages; I've heard of the standard deviation but can't say what it means; when I see p < .05 in a paper, I only know it means 'significant,' not what it actually says. A specific self-assessment helps you decide which lessons to take slowly and which prerequisites to fill in first. Come back to it once after M3 and once at the end of the course, and check whether your original worries were resolved.

SummaryThree things to take away

  • CLPN answers this question: in the same people measured several times, does the earlier score on one item predict the later score on another item, after controlling for the other items?
  • A CLPN is made of many regressions, so you first learn statistics (M1), R (M2), and regression (M3), and then longitudinal data (M4).
