Variables and Measurement: Where Data Come From

① IntuitionEvery number in a data table is a "measurement"

From a questionnaire to a row of numbers

A community social worker asked a resident, Mr. Wang, to fill in a questionnaire. For item 1, "How often do you feel that you lack companionship?", he ticked "Sometimes"; for item 6, "Over the past two weeks, on how many days have you felt down?", he ticked "Several days"; at the end he filled in his gender, "male," and his age, "72." Back at the office, the sheet was typed into Excel as one row: P017, 3, …, 1, …, 0, 72.

Notice that "Sometimes" became 3 and "male" became 0. A number does not tell you which calculations make sense with it: what does it mean to average "male = 0, female = 1"? Is "Sometimes (3)" 1.5 times "Rarely (2)"? This lesson answers the question: what does each column of numbers actually stand for, and what can you do with it?

Variables: characteristics that vary

A variable is a characteristic that takes different values for different people (or at different times): age, gender, and loneliness score are all variables. In a data table, one column = one variable and one row = one person (a case). The course dataset data/clpn_sim_wide.csv has 800 rows and 34 columns.

Four levels of measurement

The psychologist Stevens sorted numbers into four levels by how much they mean, called levels of measurement. The further down the table, the more information the numbers carry and the more calculations you can do with them:

LevelWhat the numbers meanSocial work examplesWhat you can do
nominalOnly labels, no sizeGender (0 = male, 1 = female), household registration type (hukou, China's urban/rural residence status), type of service program, participant IDCount, compute proportions
ordinalOrdered, but the gaps are not necessarily equal"Never / Rarely / Sometimes / Often," education level, satisfaction rated 1–5+ ranking, median
intervalEqual gaps, but no "true zero"Temperature in Celsius (0°C is not "no temperature"); many scale total scores are treated as interval+ adding and subtracting, mean, standard deviation
ratioEqual gaps, and 0 means "none at all"Age, monthly income, hours of sleep per night, number of home visits by a social worker+ multiplying and dividing ("twice as much" makes sense)
Analogy: a staircase

An ordinal variable is like a staircase with steps of different heights: you know step 3 is higher than step 2, but you don't know whether "2 to 3" is as high as "3 to 4." An interval variable is a staircase whose steps are all the same height. A ratio variable also has a real ground floor (a zero point), so "step 4 is twice as high as step 2" makes sense.

Likert scales: the "gray area" between ordinal and interval

A Likert scale has a few graded response options, such as "Strongly disagree … Strongly agree" or "Never … Often." Strictly speaking it is ordinal: nobody can guarantee that the psychological distance from "Rarely" to "Sometimes" equals the distance from "Sometimes" to "Often." In practice, though, researchers often:

  • treat single items as continuous and compute means and Pearson correlations directly. With enough categories (say 5 or more) and a distribution that is not too skewed, this is usually fine;
  • add several items into a total score, which takes more values and is closer to a continuous variable;
  • when an item has only 4 categories and is strongly skewed (for example, many people choose 0), play it safer and use a correlation designed for ordinal data (such as the polychoric correlation, introduced in 1.4 Covariance and Correlation: How Two Variables Co-Vary).

This is a contested methodological question with no once-and-for-all answer; in a paper, state clearly what you did and why.

Items, total scores, and constructs

Three terms that are easy to mix up:

  • construct: the abstract concept we want to measure, such as "loneliness" or "depression." You cannot see or touch it.
  • item: one concrete question on a questionnaire, such as "How often do you feel left out?" It is one concrete facet of the construct.
  • Scale score: the sum (or mean) of the items that belong to the same construct.

In the course data, loneliness is measured with 4 items (lone1–lone4: Lack of companionship, Feeling left out, Feeling isolated, No one understands me; each scored 1–4), and depression with 5 items (dep1–dep5: Depressed mood, Loss of interest, Sleep problems, Fatigue, Worthlessness; each scored 0–3, following the scoring of the PHQ-9).

This is why CLPN usually works at the item level: a total score lumps together "the person who sleeps badly" and "the person who feels worthless." A network method treats each item as a node, keeps the information about "which kind of problem it is," and lets you ask "which item predicts which item." The price is that a single item has more measurement error than a total score; Lesson 9.2 discusses this.

Reliability and validity: is the measurement any good?

  • reliability: whether the measurement is stable and consistent. If the same person is measured again two days later, are the scores close? Do the items for the same construct agree with each other? Example: if the same person steps on a scale twice in a row and it reads 60 kg one time and 65 kg the next, the readings jump around and the scale cannot be trusted.
  • validity: whether the measurement measures what it is meant to measure. Example: if you measure depression with the question "Have you often worked overtime lately?", the answers may be very stable (high reliability), but they do not measure depression (low validity). A different kind of problem is systematic bias: a bathroom scale that always shows 5 kg too much still moves up and down with your true weight, and it still gets right who is heavier than whom; it just reads high across the board. This affects whether the scale is "accurate," not whether it "measures weight." Questionnaires have the same kind of problem, for example when the wording at one wave makes everyone report a little more; the "intercept invariance" check in 7.6 Measurement Invariance and Sample Size Planning looks for exactly this kind of bias.
Reliability is a precondition for validity, not a guarantee

An unstable measurement cannot be accurate (low reliability → validity is necessarily limited); but a stable measurement is not necessarily accurate (high reliability ≠ high validity).

② Essential MathTotal scores and "true score + error"

Total scores

$$S_i = \sum_{j=1}^{4} x_{ij} = x_{i1} + x_{i2} + x_{i3} + x_{i4}$$
\(S_i\)
Person \(i\)'s loneliness total score.
\(x_{ij}\)
Person \(i\)'s score on item \(j\) (two subscripts: person first, then item).
\(\sum_{j=1}^{4}\)
Sum over the 4 items.

Each of the 4 loneliness items is scored 1–4, so the total ranges from \(4\times1=4\) to \(4\times4=16\); each of the 5 depression items is scored 0–3, so that total ranges from 0 to 15.

Classical test theory: observed score = true score + error

$$X = T + E \qquad\qquad \text{Reliability} = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(T)}{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$$
\(X\)
The score you actually observe.
\(T\)
The "true score": the score this person would get if you could measure endlessly, without error.
\(E\)
Random error: the mood of the day, misreading a question, ticking the wrong box by accident...
\(\operatorname{Var}\)
Variance: how spread out a set of scores is (next lesson).

Reliability is "the share of the differences in observed scores that comes from real differences." A reliability of 0.8 means 80% of the differences are real and 20% are noise. A common index of internal consistency is Cronbach's α; in the course data, the 4 loneliness items and the 5 depression items both have α of about 0.78 at T1 (see the R code below). The more items, the more the random errors cancel out, so a total score is usually more reliable than a single item. Item-level network analysis gives up this advantage; that is its price.

④ Concept CheckQuick quiz

What level of measurement is "gender (0 = male, 1 = female)"?

  • Correct: 0 and 1 are only labels; changing them to 1 and 2 would not change the meaning.
  • Male and female have no order of "which is higher"; 1 being larger than 0 is just a result of the coding.
  • Labels have no "gaps" between them; that R can compute a mean (= the proportion of women) doesn't make it an interval variable.
  • 0 does not mean "no gender"; it is just the code for "male."
Being coded as numbers in the data does not make a variable numeric.

Strictly speaking, what level is "satisfaction with the service (1 = very dissatisfied … 5 = very satisfied)"?

  • The options have a clear order; they are not just labels.
  • Correct: there is an order, but nobody can guarantee that the "psychological distances" between neighboring options are equal. In practice it is often treated as roughly interval.
  • In practice it is often treated this way as an approximation, but the question says "strictly speaking": nobody can guarantee that "dissatisfied" to "neutral" is the same distance as "neutral" to "satisfied."
  • There is no "true zero": 1 means "very dissatisfied," not "no satisfaction."
Likert items sit in the "gray area" between ordinal and interval; say in your paper how you handled them.

What level of measurement is "monthly income (in yuan)"?

  • Income has more than an order; the gaps are equal too (every yuan is the same).
  • The gaps are equal, yes, but it also has a true zero, so "twice as much" makes sense too.
  • Correct: 0 yuan means "no income at all," and "8,000 yuan is twice 4,000 yuan" makes sense.
Age, hours of sleep, and number of home visits are ratio variables too.

Why does CLPN usually work at the "item level" rather than with total scores?

  • The opposite is true: the more items, the more random errors cancel out, so total scores are usually more reliable than single items. That is a cost of item-level analysis, not a reason for it.
  • Correct. The price is that a single item is usually less reliable than a total score.
  • Total scores can certainly be used in regression (the CLPM works with total scores).
  • A total score only adds items of the same construct (the 4 loneliness items and the 5 depression items are summed separately); the problem is that different problems within one construct get merged.
The item level keeps information, but it also brings larger measurement error and possible overlap in item content (Lesson 9.2).

The item "Have you often worked overtime lately?" is used to measure depression. Asked again two weeks later, the same person gives almost the same answer. This item has:

  • Correct: the answers are stable (reliable), but it doesn't measure depression.
  • The answers are very stable, so reliability is not low; and it doesn't measure depression.
  • This is the misreading this lesson warns about: stable does not mean it measures what you want to measure.
  • The answer barely changes after two weeks, so it is very stable; reliability is not low.
Reliability is a necessary condition for validity, but not a sufficient one.

In the course data, each of the 4 loneliness items is scored 1–4. What is the smallest possible loneliness total score?

Each item is at least 1, and there are 4 items.
4 × 1 = 4; the maximum is 4 × 4 = 16. That is why the loneliness total in the L1-2 output ranges from 4 to 16.

In \(X = T + E\), what is the closest meaning of a reliability of 0.78?

  • Reliability describes where the differences in scores across the whole sample come from, not what one person's score is made of.
  • Correct.
  • Reliability says nothing about reading too high or too low; reading high across the board is systematic bias, which reliability doesn't cover.
  • Reliability only says how stable the measurement is, not whether it measures what it is meant to measure.
Reliability is a "proportion": true score variance / observed score variance.

⑤ ExercisesHands-on practice

Exercise 1.1-A: Label a scale

Take a widely used scale (such as the PHQ-9 depression screener or the UCLA Loneliness Scale). For each item, list: (1) its level of measurement; (2) its range of values; (3) which facet of the construct it covers (e.g., "emotional," "physical," "social"); (4) in an analysis, would you treat the single item as ordinal or as roughly interval? Give your reason in one sentence.

Example answer for the PHQ-9

The PHQ-9 has 9 items asking how often something happened over the past two weeks, each scored 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), for a total of 0–27. Each item is ordinal (4 categories); the total is often treated as roughly interval. The facets can be roughly split into emotional/cognitive (depressed mood, loss of interest, worthlessness/guilt, concentration, thoughts of death or self-harm) and physical (sleep, fatigue, appetite, being slowed down or restless). This split is a rough grouping for practice, not the scale's official factor structure.

Question (4) has no single answer: a single item with only 4 categories and a clear right skew is more safely treated as ordinal (e.g., with polychoric correlations, Lesson 1.4); a total score takes many values and is often treated as roughly interval. What matters is that you state what you did and why.

If you chose the UCLA Loneliness Scale: it has a full 20-item version and a widely used 3-item short version; say which version you used and how it is scored.

Exercise 1.1-B: Predict the "close friends" in the network

If each item is a node in a network, which 3 pairs of items do you expect to be most closely related? Write them down with your reasons. In 1.4 Covariance and Correlation: How Two Variables Co-Vary and 2.5 Descriptive Statistics, Correlation, and Regression in R you will test your guesses on the data.

Suggested reasoning and the results in the course data

Items of the same construct are usually more closely related (e.g., "Lack of companionship" and "No one understands me"), and so are items with similar content (e.g., "Sleep problems" and "Fatigue," because if you sleep badly you are tired the next day). In the course data at T1: lone1–lone4 (Lack of companionship–No one understands me) r = 0.51, dep3–dep4 (Sleep problems–Fatigue) r = 0.44, while the correlations across constructs (loneliness item × depression item) all lie between 0.12 and 0.25.

Self-check: are your reasons based on the item content? Did you notice that a strong link between "two items with almost the same content" may just mean they measure the same thing (Lesson 9.2 calls this "topological overlap")?

SummaryThree things to take away

  • What a number means depends on its level of measurement: nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio; Likert items sit in the gray area between ordinal and interval.
  • Construct (invisible) → item (a concrete question) → total score (the sum); CLPN works at the item level and keeps the information about "which kind of problem."
  • Reliability = stable and consistent; validity = measures what it should measure. Next lesson: 1.2 Distributions and Descriptive Statistics: Mean, Variance, and Standard Deviation.
