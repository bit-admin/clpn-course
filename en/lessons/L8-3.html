Case-Dropping Bootstrap and the CS-Coefficient

Study time 2 hrPrerequisites: 8.2 Edge-Weight Accuracy: bootnet and a Custom CLPN Estimation FunctionR script: R/L8-3.R

Learning Objectives

  1. Understand the case-dropping bootstrap: drop more and more of the sample and see whether the centrality ranking holds
  2. Calculate and interpret the correlation stability coefficient (CS-coefficient), with the usual reference values (not below 0.25, preferably above 0.5)
  3. Report and judge the stability of out-EI / in-EI

Core Concepts

  • case-dropping bootstrap
  • correlation stability coefficient (CS-coefficient)
  • centrality stability
  • rank-order consistency

Lesson 8.2 answered "how precise is each edge?". This lesson answers a different question: can you trust the centrality ranking? If a paper says "lone4 has the highest out-EI", readers need to know whether it would still be highest with a different group of people. The case-dropping bootstrap and the CS-coefficient were designed for exactly this.

Lesson structure: Intuition → Essential Math → R Code → Concept Check → Exercises → Summary. You can drag or click the interactive figures; a wrong quiz answer first gets a hint, and after two wrong answers you can see the explanation.

① IntuitionBuild the Mental Picture

The idea: drop more and more people

The case-dropping bootstrap works like this:

  1. Drop 5% of the people at random, re-estimate the network on the remaining 95%, compute centrality (for example out-EI), and correlate it with the centrality from the full sample.
  2. Repeat many times, and do the same for dropping 10%, 20% … up to 75% (bootnet's default maximum); bootnet divides the total number of replicates among the drop proportions.
  3. If the centrality pattern still agrees closely with the full sample after more than half the people are dropped, the ranking is stable; if dropping just a few people scrambles it, the ranking is unstable.

The CS-coefficient in one sentence

CS-coefficient (correlation stability coefficient): the largest proportion of cases you can drop while, in 95% of the subsamples, the correlation between subsample centrality and original-sample centrality stays ≥ 0.7.

Epskamp, Borsboom and Fried (2018) suggest that CS should not fall below 0.25 and preferably be above 0.5. CS = 0.6 means: even after dropping 60% of the people, you can be 95% sure that the correlation of centrality with the original sample is at least 0.7.

Can these two thresholds be used directly for a CLPN?

0.25 and 0.5 come from the simulation study of Epskamp, Borsboom and Fried (2018). They simulated cross-sectional undirected networks (partial correlation networks) and looked at undirected centrality indices such as strength, closeness and betweenness. A CLPN is a directed network; this course uses out-EI and in-EI for 9 nodes, and each node's coefficients come from a separate LASSO regression. As far as the course author knows, no study has checked whether these two thresholds are equally suitable for a directed CLPN with this few nodes.

So the course uses them as reference lines that are common in the literature, not as cutoffs with theoretical guarantees. In practice: (1) report the actual CS value, not just "passed" or "failed"; (2) in the Methods, cite where the thresholds come from and state that they come from simulations of undirected networks; (3) when CS is near a threshold (like in-EI in this lesson, between the 0.44 and 0.52 steps), treat it as a borderline case and interpret it cautiously; (4) with only 9 nodes, the correlation itself is unstable (a correlation computed from 9 points has large sampling fluctuation), which is one reason the CS result easily jumps between two steps.

The simulation below shows how the CS-coefficient is read from the curves, and how the sample size and the true differences between nodes affect it. Try to find out: at N = 300, how large must the true EI difference be for CS to reach ≥ 0.5?

Why do the true differences between nodes matter?

Whether a centrality ranking is stable depends on the size of the true differences between nodes relative to the estimation noise. If the true out-EI of all 9 nodes is about the same (all around 0.2), even a very large sample can hardly produce a stable order, because there is no real order. So a low CS does not necessarily mean the study was done badly; the nodes may simply be about equally important. Either way the conclusion is the same: do not interpret the ranking.

How to write it up when CS falls short

CS valueYou can sayDo not say
≥ 0.5"lone4 had the highest out-EI (CS = …); the ranking was fairly stable." (still prediction, not causation)"lone4 is the core symptom driving depression."
0.25–0.5"The stability of the out-EI ranking was moderate (CS = …); the following ranking is for reference only: …"Treating the top-ranked node as the main finding
< 0.25"The stability of out-EI was insufficient (CS = 0.13), so we do not interpret the out-EI ranking of the nodes and report it only in the supplementary material.""lone4 is the most influential."
The principle for scaling back claims:

When CS is insufficient, the conclusion falls back from "a ranking of nodes" to "a description of edges" (e.g., "several loneliness items have positive predictive edges to later depression items") or to "construct-level sums" (e.g., "the sum of edge weights loneliness → depression is larger than in the reverse direction", tested with the bootstrap).

Results for the course data

Why does the CS of in-EI "change from run to run"? CS is not computed on a continuous scale. It is checked at a fixed set of drop proportions (5%, 12.7%, 20.5%, 28.4%, 36.1%, 43.9%, 51.6% …), and the largest step that still meets the 95% condition is taken. In the course data, in-EI sits right between the 43.9% and 51.6% steps: depending on which subsamples are drawn (a different random seed, or parallel processing), the 51.6% step sometimes just reaches 95% (CS = 0.52) and sometimes just misses (CS = 0.44). The course script fixes the seed and runs on one core, so it gets the same value every time (currently 0.52, the same as 0.52 in Lesson 10.2); but reproducible is not the same as stable, and the value itself is still borderline. So report the actual numbers: the stability of in-EI is at the edge of 0.5, so its ranking can only be interpreted cautiously; out-EI is around 0.20 every time, consistently below the bar. For a more precise CS, follow bootnet's hint: set caseMin/caseMax to put more steps in the critical range and increase nBoots.

This result means the eye-catching finding from Lesson 7.4, "lone4 (No one understands me) has the highest out-EI", cannot go into the conclusions, while the in-EI ranking, "depression items such as dep4 (Fatigue) and dep1 (Depressed mood) are easier to predict", can be reported (cautiously). Recall from Lesson 7.4 that lone4's outgoing edges do not stand out whether computed from the population target or from the within-person truth. But keep these two things apart: CS only says that the out-EI ranking is unstable when some participants are removed. It measures how stable the estimate is relative to this sample, not how far the estimate is from the population target or the within-person truth. A biased estimate can be very stable (Lesson 10.2 compares each estimate with the population target and the within-person truth, and there are such edges there); "does not match the answer" can only be seen in simulated data where the answer is known. Even if CS is high and the ranking agrees with the population target, it may still not be the ranking of the within-person process, because EI also adds in the within-construct edges produced by the trait.

Common questions and pitfalls

What if CS is low?

① Don't interpret that index's ranking; report only the values. ② Consider a larger sample. ③ Use fewer nodes or combine redundant items. ④ Say so in the limitations. Don't quietly switch to an index with a higher CS without reporting the original one; that too is a researcher degree of freedom (Lesson 9.2).

Why is in-EI more stable than out-EI in the course data?

One possible explanation: in-EI is the "column sum" of the coefficients of a single LASSO regression, while out-EI is a "row sum" that adds up coefficients from 9 different regressions, each with its own λ and noise. In the course data the CS of in-EI is 0.52 (0.52 in the separate run of Lesson 10.2), while out-EI's is only 0.20. But this is not a general rule; other data may differ, and you have to check.

② Essential MathWrite the Intuition as Equations

\[\mathrm{CS}(\mathrm{cor}=0.7)=\max\Big\{\,d\ :\ P\big(\ \mathrm{cor}(\hat c^{\,(d)},\hat c\,)\ \ge 0.7\ \big)\ \ge 0.95\Big\}\]
SymbolReading / Meaning
\(d\)Proportion of cases dropped; by default bootnet uses 10 equally spaced steps between 0.05 and 0.75: 0.05, 0.127, 0.205, …, 0.75
\(\hat c\)Centrality vector on the full sample (e.g., the out-EI of the 9 nodes)
\(\hat c^{\,(d)}\)Centrality vector re-estimated on a subsample with drop proportion d
\(\mathrm{cor}(\cdot,\cdot)\)(Pearson) correlation between two centrality vectors, measuring agreement in pattern/ranking
\(P(\cdots)\ge0.95\)Among all subsamples at this drop proportion, at least 95% have correlation ≥ 0.7
\(\max\{\cdots\}\)The largest drop proportion that meets the condition

Why are smaller samples less stable? (intuition)

With a subsample of size \(n=(1-d)N\), the noise in each centrality estimate is roughly proportional to \(1/\sqrt{n}\). The correlation between the centrality vector and the full-sample vector depends on the ratio of the variance of the true differences between nodes to the noise variance:

\[\mathrm{cor}\approx\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2_{\text{true diff.}}}{\sigma^2_{\text{true diff.}}+\sigma^2_{\text{noise}}(n)}},\qquad \sigma^2_{\text{noise}}(n)\propto\frac1n\]

(This is a simplified formula for intuition; the interactive demo on this page simulates along exactly these lines.) The larger the true differences and the larger n, the closer the correlation is to 1.

The argument in bootnet

In corStability(cd, cor = 0.7), cor is the 0.7 in the formula; you can make it stricter, e.g., 0.8. When reporting, use the default so results can be compared with the literature, and explain any change.

Worked example: reading the CS-coefficient from a case-dropping table

Suppose a centrality index gives the case-dropping results below. Each row drops a given proportion of people at random and re-estimates the network; the second column is the proportion of bootstrap samples in which the new centrality vector correlates ≥ 0.7 with the full-sample centrality vector:

Proportion droppedProportion with correlation ≥ 0.7≥ 95%?
0.051.00Yes
0.131.00Yes
0.210.99Yes
0.280.97Yes
0.360.96Yes
0.440.93No
0.520.88No

The CS-coefficient is the largest proportion you can drop while at least 95% of samples still have a correlation ≥ 0.7. In the table, the largest proportion that meets this is 0.36, so CS = 0.36. By the usual guideline (Epskamp et al., 2018), CS ≥ 0.25 is the minimum for interpreting the ranking, and ≥ 0.5 is good. 0.36 lies in between: you can interpret the ranking with caution.

Note that CS can only take values on the grid (0.05, 0.13, …). The real cut-off may lie somewhere between 0.36 and 0.44, but 0.36 is all we can report. This is also why the CS of in-EI in the course data jumps between the two grid values 0.44 and 0.52.

Reading plot(cd): the x-axis is the proportion of the sample kept (by default bootnet decreases it from left to right), the y-axis is the mean correlation with the original-sample centrality, and the shading is the 2.5%–97.5% quantile range. The more slowly the curve falls, the more stable the index. The script also prints a verdict against the 0.25 / 0.5 thresholds automatically.

Common mistakes when reporting CS
  • Reporting CS without the correlation threshold (0.7) and the proportion (95%). These are bootnet's defaults, but state them in the methods section.
  • Ranking nodes even though CS is low. When CS < 0.25, even if you plot the centrality ranking, all you can say is "the ranking is unstable and is not interpreted".
  • Confusing the stability of edges with the CS of centrality. Case-dropping assesses how stable the centrality ranking is. It says nothing about which edges are trustworthy; for edges, look at the intervals from Lesson 8.2.

Companion Script & Output

Complete companion script R/L8-3.R (52 lines, click to expand)
# =====================================================================
# L8-3.R  Case-Dropping Bootstrap & CS-Coefficient
# (Case-dropping bootstrap & CS-coefficient)
# CS = maximum proportion of cases that can be dropped while retaining 95%
# confidence that centrality correlates with the original sample at r >= 0.7
# Benchmark guidelines: CS should not fall below 0.25, and preferably exceed 0.5
# (Epskamp, Borsboom & Fried, 2018)
# =====================================================================
source("R/_setup.R")
suppressPackageStartupMessages({ library(bootnet); library(glmnet) })
log_open("L8-3_log.txt")
dat <- read_course_data()
wide_items <- dat[, c(vars_t(1), vars_t(2))]
# Reproducibility: Parallel bootnet (nCores > 1) resamples across worker processes using unseeded RNGs,
# so results drift slightly across runs even with set.seed(). Therefore, we default to single-core (nCores = 1)
# where set.seed() guarantees exact reproducibility (about 5–7x slower).
# If you only want a quick run and do not need bitwise reproducibility, set to max(1, parallel::detectCores() - 1).
n_boots <- 1000; n_cores <- 1
clpn_fun <- function(data) {                       # Identical estimation function as in L8-2
  p <- ncol(data) / 2
  X <- scale(as.matrix(data[, 1:p])); Y <- scale(as.matrix(data[, (p + 1):(2 * p)]))
  B <- matrix(0, p, p)
  for (j in seq_len(p)) {
    cvfit <- glmnet::cv.glmnet(X, Y[, j], alpha = 1, nfolds = 10)
    B[, j] <- as.numeric(coef(cvfit, s = "lambda.min"))[-1]
  }
  B
}
set.seed(2026)
net <- estimateNetwork(wide_items, default = "none", fun = clpn_fun, directed = TRUE, labels = items)

# ---- Course Code ----
set.seed(2026)
cd <- bootnet(net, nBoots = n_boots, type = "case", nCores = n_cores,
              statistics = c("outExpectedInfluence", "inExpectedInfluence"), verbose = FALSE)
cs <- corStability(cd); cat("\n")
saveRDS(cd, out("L8-3_casedrop.rds"))
write.csv(data.frame(statistic = names(cs), CS = round(cs, 3)), out("L8-3_CS.csv"), row.names = FALSE)
png_open("L8-3_casedrop.png", 1600, 1200)
suppressWarnings(print(plot(cd, statistics = c("outExpectedInfluence", "inExpectedInfluence"))))
invisible(dev.off())
# Evaluation guidelines have only two thresholds: 0.25 and 0.5 (Epskamp et al., 2018). CS values can only
# land on the grid of drop proportions (bootnet defaults to 10 levels from 0.05 to 0.75, spacing ~0.08).
# Therefore, when barely exceeding 0.5, note explicitly: varying the random seed may cause it to drop to the next level.
step <- diff(seq(0.05, 0.75, length.out = 10))[1]
for (k in names(cs)) {
  verdict <- if (cs[k] >= .5 + step) "stable (>= 0.5); centrality rankings can be interpreted" else if (cs[k] >= .5)
    "just reaches 0.5 (one grid step above cutoff): marginally stable; altering seed may drop to lower step; interpret rankings with caution" else if (cs[k] >= .25)
    "minimally acceptable (0.25-0.5); interpret rankings with caution" else "unstable (< 0.25); do not interpret rankings"
  cat(sprintf("%-22s CS = %.2f -> %s\n", k, cs[k], verdict))
}
log_close()

④ Concept CheckInstant Check

What does CS = 0.6 mean?

  • CS is about centrality indices, not edges; for the precision of edges, see the intervals of Lesson 8.2.
  • Correct.
  • Plausible but wrong: 0.6 is the drop proportion, not a correlation; the correlation threshold is the 0.7 in the formula.
  • The requirement is that at least 95% of subsamples have correlation ≥ 0.7, not an identical ranking.

If the CS of out-EI is only 0.13, can a paper still call some node "the most influential"?

  • "Clearly higher in the sample" is exactly what needs to be checked; CS = 0.13 means the ranking may change after dropping only a few cases.
  • Correct.
  • A centrality ranking has no corresponding p-value; significance and the stability of a ranking are two different things.
  • A large sample does not guarantee a stable ranking: the course data have N = 800, and the CS of out-EI is still below 0.25.

How do the questions answered by the case-dropping and the ordinary bootstrap differ?

  • Both the sampling and the question differ (see the comparison table in this lesson).
  • Correct.
  • Roughly the reverse: the precision of edges comes from the ordinary bootstrap, the stability of the centrality ranking from case-dropping.
  • Case-dropping draws a subset of cases without replacement.

What is the usual reference standard for CS?

  • 0.05 is the first step of bootnet's grid of drop proportions, not a standard.
  • Correct (Epskamp et al., 2018); for a directed CLPN it is only a reference.
  • 0.95 is the "at least 95% of subsamples" requirement, not the CS standard.
  • 0.7 is the correlation threshold used in the computation, not the CS standard.

Which situation is most likely to give a very low CS even with a large sample?

  • Correct: with no true differences, there is no stable order to find.
  • The number of replicates affects how precise CS is (it may jump between two adjacent steps); it does not make a stable index look very unstable.
  • What is dropped is a proportion; with a larger sample, more people remain at the same proportion, so the ranking can only be more stable.
  • Standardization changes the scale of the coefficients, not the size of the true differences between nodes; the items are already standardized, and when node differences are small CS is low all the same.

⑤ ExercisesHands-On Practice

Exercise 1: CS-coefficients and scaled-back wordingHands-on + writing

Run R/L8-3.R and report the CS-coefficients of out-EI and in-EI. If CS < 0.25, write a paragraph on how the conclusions should be scaled back; if ≥ 0.25, write how the ranking can be reported.

Show Reference Solution / Walkthrough

Course data (current output): CS of in-EI = 0.52, CS of out-EI = 0.20, so out-EI must be scaled back; in-EI is near 0.5 (0.52 with the fixed seed; with other random seeds it varies between the 0.44 and 0.52 steps), which is borderline stable, so its ranking can only be reported cautiously. If CS < 0.25: "The case-dropping bootstrap showed that the stability of out-EI was insufficient (CS = …), so we do not interpret the out-EI ranking of the nodes. At the edge level, several loneliness items (such as 'No one understands me') have positive cross-lagged edges to T2 depression items, but their bootstrap intervals are fairly wide." If you also want a construct-level comparison ("the sum of edge weights loneliness → depression is larger than in the reverse direction"), test this difference first; do not just see which of the two sums is bigger. How: read in R/output/L8-2_boot.rds, saved in Lesson 8.2, compute Σ B[loneliness, depression] − Σ B[depression, loneliness] in each bootstrap network, and take the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of the difference (code in part 2 of R/solutions/L8-3_solution.R, results written to R/output/sol_L8-3_log.txt). Only if the interval excludes 0 can you write "the sum of edge weights in the loneliness → depression direction is larger than in the reverse direction"; if it contains 0, write "no difference was detected between the sums of edge weights in the two directions". Course data: the loneliness → depression sum is 0.557, depression → loneliness is 0.079, and the difference 0.478 has a 95% bootstrap interval of [0.121, 0.832], which excludes 0, so the sentence can be written for these data; it is still a predictive relationship, and the interval is wide. Also remember that with signed sums, positive and negative edges cancel out, so a small sum does not mean weak connections. If 0.25–0.5: report the ranking with a "for reference only" qualifier; if ≥ 0.5: you can report the ranking, still without causal words.

Exercise 2: Finding the conditions in the demoUnderstanding

In the CS simulation on this page, find the smallest "true EI difference" (the "True EI spread across nodes" slider) that gives CS ≥ 0.5 at N = 150, 500 and 3000. Summarize the pattern.

Show Reference Solution / Walkthrough

The larger N, the smaller the true difference needed (roughly proportional to 1/√N). At N = 150 you need a large difference; at N = 3000 a fairly small difference can also be ranked stably. The pattern: stability = signal (true difference) / noise (∝ 1/√N). Exact values vary slightly with the simulation seed.

SummaryKey Takeaways & Further Reading

  • Case-dropping: drop more and more cases, re-estimate centrality, compare with the full sample.
  • CS = the largest proportion that can be dropped while 95% of subsamples keep correlation ≥ 0.7.
  • Reference: < 0.25 do not interpret the ranking; 0.25–0.5 with caution; ≥ 0.5 fairly stable.
  • A low CS may come from a small sample or from small true differences between nodes.
  • CS insufficient → fall back to edge-level or construct-level description.
