## ① Intuition "With Everything Else Equal"

**How much does loneliness predict "on top of" everything else?**

You predict the T2 depression total from the T1 loneliness total and get a slope of 0.32: for each 1 point higher on T1 loneliness, T2 depression is 0.32 points higher on average. Your supervisor presses: "Lonely people also tend to sleep badly and have less social support. How much of that 0.32 really belongs to sleep and social support? And people who are depressed at T1 are mostly still depressed at T2, and people depressed at T1 also tend to be lonely. Could what you see just be 'already depressed'?" To answer these questions, you need to put several predictors into the model at once: multiple regression.

### Partial regression coefficients: "holding the other variables constant"

Multiple regression gives each predictor a coefficient, called a partial regression coefficient. It means: comparing people who are the same on all the other predictors, how much the outcome differs on average when this variable is 1 unit higher. The variables put into the model to be "held constant" are called covariates (control variables).

**Analogy: compare only people in the same situation**

Imagine sorting the 800 people into many small groups by sleep duration and social support, so that within each group everyone has almost the same sleep and social support. Look at the relationship between loneliness and T2 depression inside each group, then combine the results across groups: that is the coefficient of loneliness "after controlling for sleep and social support." Multiple regression does this in one step with mathematics, without actually forming groups.

In the course data ([R/L3-2.R](../R/L3-2.R)), the coefficient of loneliness changes from model to model:

| Model | Predictors of T2 depression | Coefficient of T1 loneliness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| m1 | T1 loneliness only | 0.324 |
| m2 | T1 loneliness + social support + sleep duration | 0.282 |
| m3 | T1 loneliness + T1 depression | 0.140 |

Adding social support and sleep makes the coefficient only slightly smaller; adding T1 depression cuts it to less than half. The explanation: T1 loneliness is correlated with T1 depression (r = 0.31), and T1 depression is by far the strongest predictor of T2 depression (people's depression is fairly stable). In m1, the coefficient of loneliness "borrowed" part of the predictive power that belongs to T1 depression; m3 gives it back. The 0.14 that remains is what loneliness adds in predicting T2 depression "among people with the same T1 depression."

### Why does a coefficient change after controlling?

(Interactive widget: partial)

The simulation above shows a rule (all variables standardized):

$$\text{coefficient of loneliness in simple regression} = \text{unique effect of loneliness} + \text{effect of sleep} \times \rho(\text{loneliness},\text{sleep})$$

If you leave out a variable that is correlated with loneliness and also affects depression (here, sleep problems), the coefficient of loneliness "absorbs" part of its effect. This is called omitted variable bias, the regression version of "confounding" from Lesson 1.4. Put the variable into the model (control for it) and this part of the bias disappears. Drag ρ to 0 and the two coefficients become equal: if two predictors are uncorrelated, controlling for one does not change the other's coefficient.

**More controls is not always better**

"Controlling" handles only the variables you measured and put into the model; unmeasured confounders are still there. Also, if you control for a variable in the middle of a causal chain (for example loneliness → worse sleep → depression, and you control for sleep), you also "control away" the part of loneliness's effect that runs through sleep; a smaller coefficient then does not mean loneliness is unimportant. What to control for should follow from the research question and theory, not from putting in every variable you can find.

### The link to CLPN: one edge = one coefficient that controls for all other T1 variables

Model m3 is a "mini CLPM":

$$\text{depression}_{T2} = b_0 + \underbrace{0.534}_{\text{autoregressive}}\times\text{depression}_{T1} + \underbrace{0.140}_{\text{cross-lagged}}\times\text{loneliness}_{T1}$$

CLPN scales this up to the item level: when predicting T2 Depressed mood, for example, all 9 T1 items go on the right-hand side at once (including T1 Depressed mood itself). So the edge "T1 Feeling isolated → T2 Depressed mood" means: among people who are the same at T1 on the other 8 items (including Depressed mood itself), how much higher T2 Depressed mood is on average when Feeling isolated is 1 unit higher. This property of "controlling for all other T1 nodes" is the basic difference between a CLPN edge and a simple correlation: it removes confounding by the other **measured** T1 items.

That does not make CLPN edges "clean." One kind of confounding cannot be removed however many T1 items you control for: each person's long-term stable trait. Some people, for instance, are consistently lonelier than others and also consistently more prone to low mood. Such stable individual differences make "people high on T1 loneliness are also high on T2 depression" true even if, within one person, rises and falls in loneliness are not followed by rises and falls in depression. The T1 items are only an imperfect measure of this trait; putting them into the regression absorbs only part of the trait's influence, and the rest still leaks into the cross-lagged coefficients. The course data were generated exactly this way: `data/true_B_within.csv` is the within-person effect used to generate the data, and `data/true_B_population.csv` is what a standard CLPN actually estimates in the population (the population target). The two tables differ, and the difference comes from the stable traits. Lesson 5.3 (RI-CLPM) shows how to separate between-person and within-person variation.

### Multicollinearity: when two predictors are nearly the same

When two predictors are highly correlated, the model struggles to tell "whose credit it is." This is called multicollinearity. The coefficients are not necessarily biased, but they become very unstable: the standard errors grow, and the coefficients can swing up and down or even change sign. An extreme example (R code below): copy the loneliness total, add a tiny bit of noise (correlation 0.995), and put both into the model. The coefficient of loneliness goes from 0.32 to 1.28, the copy gets −0.96, and the standard error balloons from 0.04 to 0.37. The sum of the two coefficients (≈ 0.32) does not change: the model knows the total contribution of "loneliness-type stuff" but cannot split it between two almost identical variables.

The usual diagnostic is the variance inflation factor, VIF (see the math section). As a rule of thumb, VIF > 5 or 10 deserves attention (the cutoff differs between textbooks). In m2 on the course data, the VIF of loneliness is ≈ 1.23, no problem. In a CLPN, items often have similar content and are highly correlated with each other; that is one reason LASSO regularization helps (Lesson 3.5), and one reason node selection needs care (Lesson 9.2).

### Interactions (a brief introduction)

An interaction means that the effect of one variable depends on the level of another. For example, how strongly loneliness predicts depression may differ between men and women. In R you write `y ~ x1 * x2` (the same as `x1 + x2 + x1:x2`), and the coefficient of the interaction term says "how much the slope of x1 changes for each 1-unit increase in x2." A standard CLPN has no interaction terms; here you only need to know the concept exists.

## ② Essential Math The Multiple Regression Equation and VIF

$$\hat y = b_0 + b_1x_1 + b_2x_2 + \cdots + b_px_p$$

\(x_1,\dots,x_p\)The \(p\) predictors.\(b_j\)The partial regression coefficient of predictor \(j\): with the other \(x\) fixed, each 1-unit increase in \(x_j\) changes \(\hat y\) by \(b_j\). In calculus terms, \(b_j = \partial \hat y / \partial x_j\), exactly the partial derivative that "moves only one variable."

It is solved the same way as simple regression: make \(\sum (y_i-\hat y_i)^2\) as small as possible by setting the partial derivative with respect to each \(b_j\) to 0, which gives \(p+1\) equations (solved at once with matrices; R does it for you).

### Another way to see "controlling": remove the overlap first, then regress

The Frisch–Waugh theorem: \(b_1\) equals the result of two steps. (1) Predict \(x_1\) from the other predictors and keep the residuals (the part of \(x_1\) the other variables cannot explain); (2) predict \(y\) from these residuals. So a partial regression coefficient uses only the "unique part" of \(x_1\). In the course data, predicting T2 depression from "the residuals of T1 loneliness regressed on T1 depression" gives a slope of exactly 0.1398, identical to the coefficient of loneliness in m3.

### The variance inflation factor

$$VIF_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2}$$

\(R_j^2\) is the R² from "predicting \(x_j\) from all the other predictors." The better the other variables explain \(x_j\), the smaller its unique part, the closer \(R_j^2\) is to 1, the larger the VIF, and the variance (SE²) of \(b_j\) is multiplied by the VIF. Example: with \(R_j^2 = 0.9\), VIF = 10, and the SE is \(\sqrt{10}\approx 3.2\) times larger.

## ③ R Code Comparing Three Models

Script for this lesson: [R/L3-2.R](../R/L3-2.R).

R/L3-2.R (excerpt)

```r
source("R/_setup.R")
dat <- read_course_data()
dat$lone_sum_t1 <- rowSums(dat[, paste0(lone_items, "_t1")])
dat$dep_sum_t1  <- rowSums(dat[, paste0(dep_items, "_t1")])
dat$dep_sum_t2  <- rowSums(dat[, paste0(dep_items, "_t2")])
m1 <- lm(dep_sum_t2 ~ lone_sum_t1, data = dat)                                  # loneliness only
m2 <- lm(dep_sum_t2 ~ lone_sum_t1 + social_support_t1 + sleep_hours_t1, data = dat)
m3 <- lm(dep_sum_t2 ~ lone_sum_t1 + dep_sum_t1, data = dat)                     # controlling for T1 depression!
cat("Loneliness coefficients: alone", round(coef(m1)[2], 3), "; +support+sleep", round(coef(m2)[2], 3),
    "; +T1 depression (cross-lagged)", round(coef(m3)[2], 3), "\n")
summary(m3)
```

"Controlling" in two steps (Frisch–Waugh)

```r
ex <- resid(lm(lone_sum_t1 ~ dep_sum_t1, data = dat))   # the part of loneliness unrelated to T1 depression
round(coef(lm(dat$dep_sum_t2 ~ ex))[2], 4)              # = the coefficient of loneliness in m3
```

The two-step result, 0.1398, is exactly the coefficient of `lone_sum_t1` in m3, 0.13980: "controlling for T1 depression" in multiple regression means first subtracting the part of loneliness that T1 depression can explain, and then predicting with what is left.

## ④ Concept Check Quick Quiz

Why does the coefficient of T1 loneliness for T2 depression drop from 0.32 to 0.14 once T1 depression is added as a control?

- Because adding a variable reduced the sample size

- The sample size did not change (800 people in both, no missing values).

- Because T1 loneliness is correlated with T1 depression, which strongly predicts T2 depression; in the loneliness-only model, the coefficient included a part borrowed from T1 depression (Correct)

- Correct: this is omitted variable bias being removed.

- Because loneliness and depression are actually unrelated

- After controlling, a significant coefficient of 0.14 remains (p < .001).

- Because T1 depression and T1 loneliness are highly collinear, which pushes the coefficient down

- They correlate only r = 0.31, and the VIF is small; the coefficient drops because loneliness alone had "borrowed" T1 depression's predictive power, not because of collinear instability.

That is why cross-lagged paths must control for the autoregressive path.

Two predictors are completely uncorrelated (ρ = 0). If you control for one in a multiple regression, what happens to the other's coefficient?

- It does not change (Correct)

- Correct: with no overlap, there is nothing to take away.

- It becomes 0

- Controlling takes away only the overlapping part; with no overlap, the coefficient stays as it was.

- It gets smaller, because a control variable always takes away some predictive power

- It takes some away only when the two predictors are correlated; at ρ = 0 there is no overlap. If the control also predicts the outcome, adding it can even shrink the residuals and make the SE smaller.

In "simple coefficient = unique effect + other effect × ρ," the two are equal when ρ = 0.

What does multicollinearity mainly make unstable?

- The average (expected value) of the coefficient, pushing it systematically too high or too low

- Collinearity by itself usually does not bias the coefficient; it affects precision.

- The standard error grows, and the estimate swings widely from sample to sample (even changing sign) (Correct)

- Correct.

- It always makes R² smaller

- Collinearity does not necessarily lower R²; in the demonstration, adding the copy left the model's overall predictive power almost unchanged.

In the demonstration the SE grows from 0.037 to about 0.37, about 10 times (VIF about 100 → SE about 10 times larger).

A variable x₃ is explained by the other predictors with R² = 0.75. What is its VIF?

Check

VIF = 1 / (1 − R²).

1 / 0.25 = 4: the variance of its coefficient is 4 times larger, and its SE twice as large.

Reference answer: 4 (tolerance ±0.01)

Why does it matter that CLPN "controls for all other T1 variables"?

- Because it proves causation

- Unmeasured confounders remain; this lesson also points out that confounding by stable traits cannot be removed however many T1 items you control for.

- Because each edge then reflects the unique prediction among people who are the same on the other T1 items, removing confounding by the measured items (including the target item's own T1 level) (Correct)

- Correct.

- Because it removes between-person confounding by stable traits

- This is the misreading the lesson warns about: T1 items measure the trait only imperfectly and absorb only part of its influence, which is why the population target differs from the within-person effect (Lesson 5.3).

The target item's own T1 level (the autoregressive path) is included, so a cross-lagged edge is prediction "beyond stability."

A researcher wants the "total predictive effect" of loneliness on depression but controls for "sleep problems" (assume loneliness → worse sleep → depression). What happens?

- A more accurate total effect

- Controlling for a mediator cuts the path loneliness → sleep → depression, so the result is no longer the total effect.

- The part of loneliness that works through sleep is "controlled away," and the coefficient is smaller than the total predictive effect (Correct)

- Correct: don't control for variables in the middle of the causal chain (mediators), unless you want the "direct effect."

- Nothing changes

- Loneliness is correlated with sleep and sleep predicts depression, so controlling for sleep must change the coefficient of loneliness.

- The coefficient gets larger, because the noise from sleep is removed

- Here sleep is a channel through which loneliness works, not noise; controlling for it takes that part of the prediction away, so the coefficient shrinks.

The choice of control variables needs a theoretical reason.

#### Exercise 3.2-A: How the coefficient changes

Run `R/L3-2.R`. Predict T2 depression from loneliness alone, and from "loneliness + social support + sleep," compare how the coefficient of loneliness changes, and write a 150-word explanation. Then add one more model, "loneliness + social support + sleep + T1 depression": what does the coefficient become?

Reference code and key points

```r
m4 <- lm(dep_sum_t2 ~ lone_sum_t1 + social_support_t1 + sleep_hours_t1 + dep_sum_t1, data = dat)
round(coef(m4), 3)
```

Key points: 0.324 → 0.282 is only a small drop. By the formula "simple coefficient = unique effect + other effect × correlation," how much is taken away depends on two things. Social support is fairly strongly correlated with loneliness (r = −0.42), but once controlled, its coefficient for T2 depression is not significant; sleep's coefficient is significant (−0.37), but sleep is only weakly correlated with loneliness (r = −0.12). Neither is both strongly correlated and strongly predictive, so not much is taken away. With T1 depression added (m4), the coefficient of loneliness falls to about 0.128, close to the 0.140 in m3: what really takes away the predictive power is T1 depression. In your write-up, avoid "loneliness causes depression"; write instead "after controlling for …, T1 loneliness still uniquely predicted T2 depression."

Write an item-level regression: with T2 `dep1_t2` (Depressed mood) as the outcome and all 9 T1 items as predictors (`dep1_t2 ~ lone1_t1 + … + dep5_t1`). Which T1 loneliness item has the largest coefficient? How does it compare with the `dep1` column of `data/true_B_population.csv` (the population target)? source("R/_setup.R")


Result (standardized coefficients): among the loneliness items, the largest OLS estimate is `lone4_t1` (0.090), followed by `lone3_t1` (0.081). ## Summary Three Takeaways

- A partial regression coefficient compares people who are the same on the other predictors; when predictors are correlated, adding a control variable changes the coefficient (omitted variable bias).

- Multicollinearity makes coefficients unstable and SEs large; VIF = 1/(1 − R²ⱼ).

- Each CLPN edge is a partial regression coefficient that controls for all other T1 nodes (including the autoregressive one). Next lesson: [3.3 Standardized Coefficients and Results Interpretation](L3-3.html).
