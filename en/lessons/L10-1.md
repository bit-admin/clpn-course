### Step 1: ask a good research question

A good CLPN research question has three features:

1. **Not causal:** a CLPN estimates "predictive relationships over time, controlling for all T1 items", not causal effects. So do not write "loneliness causes depression"; write "to what extent do T1 loneliness items uniquely predict T2 depression items".

2. **At the item level:** the value of network analysis is that you can see *which* item matters. A good question gets down to specific symptoms, for example "is 'No one understands me' the main bridge from loneliness to depression?"

3. **Two-directional:** a cross-lagged design can test both directions as a matter of course, so the research question should include "and vice versa".

| Wording | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| Does loneliness cause depression? | Causal wording; variable level |
| Are loneliness and depression related? | Too broad; cross-sectional data could answer it |
| To what extent do T1 loneliness items uniquely predict T2 depression items, and vice versa? Which items are the main bridges? | Not causal, item level, two-directional |

### Step 3: attrition analysis

Almost every longitudinal study loses people along the way. The key question is: are the people who leave like the people who stay? In the attrition example data in L10-1.R, the retention rate is 0.80, but the mean T1 depression total of the dropouts (8.78) is far higher than that of the stayers (2.60). If you analyze only those who stayed, the sample is systematically "healthier": the T2 means are underestimated, item variances shrink (range restriction, Lesson 1.4), and the standardized edge weights, the edges LASSO selects and the stability are all affected. In this example, dropping out depends only on T1 depression, and T1 depression is itself among the predictors of every regression, so the unstandardized regression slopes are in theory still unbiased; if dropping out depended on the unobserved T2 state (MNAR), the bias would be larger (Lesson 4.3). Report the difference, and consider methods such as multiple imputation (Module 4).

### Step 4: a one-page analysis plan

Why write it before seeing results? Because a CLPN involves many "reasonable choices": the λ rule, the seed, whether to add covariates, whether to drop redundant items … If you choose after seeing the results, it is hard not to be swayed by which result looks better ("researcher degrees of freedom", Lesson 9.2). Writing the choices down in advance, with a date, is a simple form of preregistration.

### Step 5: make the full procedure a required sensitivity analysis

The main analysis in this course is the "LASSO-only" CLPN most common in the applied literature: the coefficients selected and already shrunk by LASSO are used directly as edge weights (Lessons 7.2–7.4). The methods paper that proposed the CLPN (Wysocki et al., 2025) does not recommend stopping there. Its recommended full procedure ([7.5 Design Choices and Extensions: Multi-Wave Data, Covariates, and Within-Person Disentanglement](L7-5.html)) uses LASSO only to decide which paths are fixed to 0, then re-estimates the remaining paths without a penalty in a structural equation model, tests whether the paths are equal across intervals, and finally prunes at p < .01. LASSO alone has three problems:

1. **Many false positives.** The λ chosen by cross-validation is on the small side and keeps quite a few edges that are close to 0 in the population, and this false-positive rate does not fall as the sample grows. In the course data, lambda.min keeps 19 cross-lagged edges at T1→T2 with |B| < 0.05 in the population (the same definition as the recovery table in Lesson 7.2).

2. **Coefficients are pulled toward 0.** The LASSO penalty also shrinks the coefficients it keeps, so LASSO coefficients cannot be taken directly as effect sizes.

3. **No standard errors, tests or fit indices.** LASSO gives only a set of point estimates. Only after re-estimation in SEM do you get a standard error and p-value for each path, overall fit indices (χ², CFI, TLI, RMSEA, SRMR), and a test of whether the paths are equal in the two intervals.

Then why not make the full procedure the main analysis? First, most of the published studies you will read and compare with use LASSO only; a main analysis that matches them can be compared with them directly. Second, the edge-weight bootstrap and centrality stability (CS-coefficient) of M8 are built on the LASSO estimate, and the full procedure has no ready-made counterpart. Third, the two answer slightly different questions: LASSO selects "edges that help prediction" (judged by cross-validation error), while the full procedure keeps "paths whose unpenalized estimate differs significantly from 0 at α = .01". Their biases also run in opposite directions: LASSO tends to report too many edges (high sensitivity, many false positives); the full procedure tends to report too few (few false positives, but it misses quite a few weak edges). Only with both side by side can readers see which conclusions do not depend on the estimation method.

So the capstone project in this course requires: keep LASSO for the main analysis; run the full procedure as a required sensitivity analysis (`R/L10-2b.R`), write it into the analysis plan in advance, and report it next to the main analysis in the results (Lesson 10.3). "Required" means you report it whether or not the results agree; you cannot mention it only when the two agree.

#### Full-procedure settings to fix in the plan

| Setting | This course's choice | Why fix it in advance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| λ rule and seed for edge selection | Same as the main analysis (lambda.min, set.seed(2026)) | A different rule changes which paths enter the SEM, which amounts to a different model |
| Rule for combining the intervals | Conservative rule: a path is fixed to 0 only if it is 0 in both T1→T2 and T2→T3 | The alternative (fix it if it is 0 in either interval) is sparser; the two give different results |
| Same-wave part | All residuals within a wave correlated pairwise | Absorbs same-period effects and common causes within an interval; leaving them out assumes the residuals of items in the same wave are uncorrelated, which is almost certainly false, so the model fits very badly and the standard errors and tests are wrong |
| Missing data | FIML (lavaan's missing = "ml") | LASSO cannot handle missing data (you must first drop incomplete cases or impute, Lesson 4.3), FIML can; the two analyses may use different cases, so state this in the Methods |
| Choice of final model | Use the constrained model if the nested χ² test for equality constraints gives p ≥ .05 and neither AIC nor BIC argues against it; otherwise use the unconstrained model | Picking the model after seeing the path coefficients is a researcher degree of freedom |
| Pruning criterion | p < .01 (the criterion in the original paper's example) | With .05 a few more edges are kept; a different criterion gives a different number of edges |
| Node-level summary | In-/out-prediction (proportion of variance explained) | Its unit differs from expected influence, so the two cannot be ranked together |

#### Decide in advance what to do when the two analyses disagree

The two analyses will almost certainly disagree on some edges. Deciding which to believe after seeing the results brings back researcher degrees of freedom. So the plan needs an interpretation rule. This course's rule:

- Edges kept by both analyses with the same sign are reported as main findings;

- Edges that are nonzero only in the main analysis and not kept by the full procedure are described as "sensitive to the estimation method", interpreted only as exploratory, and not used as a basis for practice recommendations;

- Edges kept by the full procedure but 0 in the main analysis are likewise reported only as exploratory. This can happen because the paths entering the SEM are the union of the two LASSO results, and the equality model also pools the information of the two intervals;

- Construct-level conclusions (e.g., "the sum of edge weights in the loneliness → depression direction is larger than in the reverse direction") go into the abstract and practical implications only if their direction agrees in both analyses.

The rule can be set differently, for example by relaxing the pruning criterion to .05. What matters is that it is fixed before you see the results and followed in the report; if a change is truly needed, list what was changed and why.

**A social work perspective**

The best research questions come from puzzles in practice. Say you notice while meeting residents at a community service center that some say "no one understands me" and others say "I have no one to keep me company". Which deserves attention first? This can be turned into a predictive question: "Which loneliness item most strongly and uniquely predicts later depression (highest bridge out-expected influence)?" On its own it cannot answer "which one should be the first target of intervention" (centrality is not an intervention target, Lesson 9.2), but it can generate hypotheses for later intervention research. You can also open your talk with this practice question.

### Common mistakes

- **Running first, writing the plan afterwards:** such a plan is no plan.

- **Too many nodes:** the more nodes, the larger the sample needed, and it grows fast (the number of coefficients is p²). There is no accepted standard for "so many people per node"; the course data have N = 800 and use only 9 nodes. If you want more nodes, first check N/p with formula 3 in the Essential Math section, then use the simulation of Lesson 7.6 to check whether the key edges can be recovered.

- **Ignoring overlapping content:** "feeling low" and "feeling down" are practically the same item; merge them or keep one (Lesson 9.2). This is also step 1 of the full procedure.

- **Treating the full procedure as a "fallback when the results look bad":** if you look at the LASSO results before deciding whether to run the full procedure, or pick the nicer of the two results as the main analysis, the sensitivity analysis loses its meaning. Fix in the plan which one is the main analysis and which is the sensitivity analysis.

## ② Essential Math Write the Intuition as Equations

This lesson has only a few small formulas, all about data preparation and checking the design.

#### 1. Reverse scoring

\[x^{\text{rev}} = (x_{\max}+x_{\min}) - x\]

| Symbol | Reading / Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| \(x\) | Original score |
| \(x_{\max}, x_{\min}\) | Highest and lowest possible scores of the scale (e.g., 4 and 1 for a 1–4 scale) |
| \(x^{\text{rev}}\) | Reverse-scored value: 1↔4, 2↔3 |

Example: on a 1–4 scale, an original 1 → (4+1)−1 = 4. The course data have no reverse-scored items, but real scales (such as some versions of the UCLA Loneliness Scale) often do.

#### 2. Retention rate

\[\text{retention rate} = \frac{N_{t}}{N_{1}}\]

| Symbol | Reading / Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| \(N_1\) | Sample size at wave 1 |
| \(N_t\) | Number of people still with data at wave \(t\) |

Example: 640 / 800 = 0.80. Then compare the T1 means of stayers and dropouts (or run a t-test) to show whether attrition is related to the study variables.

#### 3. A rough check of parameters against sample size

Each T2 node's LASSO regression has \(p\) predictors (\(p\) is the number of nodes). The whole network has at most \(p^2\) coefficients, \(p\) of them autoregressive and \(p(p-1)\) cross-lagged. In this course \(p = 9\), 81 coefficients in all; N = 800, N/p ≈ 89, so in each regression the sample is nearly 90 times the number of predictors. N/p has no accepted hard minimum; it is only a rough quick check (Lesson 9.1). If you have only N = 150 but want 20 nodes (N/p = 7.5), each regression has very little information, and the network will be very sparse and very unstable. When planning your own study, how large a sample you need has to be settled by simulation: assume a plausible true network (from pilot data or published estimates, including the weak edges), then see whether the key edges can be recovered at different N. Constantin, Schuurman and Vermunt (2026) give the general form of this Monte Carlo method; Lesson 7.6 does a simplified version.

#### 4. Number of parameters in the full procedure and the degrees of freedom of the equality test

In the path model of the full procedure, each interval (T1→T2 or T2→T3) has \(p\) autoregressive paths and \(K\) cross-lagged paths that enter the SEM. The unconstrained model estimates the two intervals separately, \(2(p+K)\) path parameters in all; the equality constraints make each path share one parameter across the two intervals, leaving \(p+K\). The other parameters (variances and covariances at T1, residual variances and same-wave residual covariances at each wave, intercepts) are the same in both models. So the degrees of freedom of the nested χ² test are:

\[\Delta df = 2(p+K) - (p+K) = p + K,\qquad \Delta\chi^2 = \chi^2_{\text{constrained}} - \chi^2_{\text{unconstrained}}\]

| Symbol | Reading / Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| \(p\) | Number of nodes (9 in the course data) |
| \(K\) | Number of cross-lagged paths (per interval) entering the SEM under the conservative rule |
| \(\Delta\chi^2\) | The extra misfit of the constrained model over the unconstrained model; if the paths are equal, it approximately follows a χ² distribution with \(\Delta df\) degrees of freedom |

In the course data \(K\) = 67, so \(\Delta df\) = 9 + 67 = 76 (the log of R/L10-2b.R prints this number, so you can use it to check that the model is written correctly). AIC and BIC are both "misfit + a penalty for the number of parameters":

\[\mathrm{AIC} = -2\log L + 2q,\qquad \mathrm{BIC} = -2\log L + q\ln N\]

| Symbol | Reading / Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| \(\log L\) | Log-likelihood of the model |
| \(q\) | Number of free parameters in the model |
| \(N\) | Sample size |

Smaller is better. The constrained model has \(p+K\) fewer parameters, so as long as its extra misfit is not too large, AIC and BIC will favor it; BIC's penalty grows with \(\ln N\), so it favors the simpler model more than AIC does. Use the constrained model only when none of the three criteria (χ² test, AIC, BIC) argues against equality, and write this rule into the plan in advance.

### Worked example: from a vague interest to a question you can analyze

Many capstone projects start with one sentence: "I want to study loneliness and depression among community residents." Here is how to narrow it step by step into a question a CLPN can answer:

1. **Pin down who and where:** "adult residents (18 and over) of communities in one city" is more specific than "community residents". Where will the sample come from? Is there existing longitudinal data?

2. **Pin down time:** a CLPN needs at least two waves, preferably three (one interval for the main analysis, one for replication). How long is the interval? It should match the process you care about: loneliness affecting mood may take months, not days.

3. **Pin down the nodes:** which items from which scales? The number of items p should not be too large. A CLPN has p × p coefficients: 9 nodes give 81. There is no accepted hard rule like "N must be at least so many times p"; N/p is only a rough quick check. Decide the sample size you need by simulation (Constantin et al., 2026; Lesson 7.6 is a simplified version), then check stability afterwards with the bootstrap and CS.

4. **Write it as a testable question:** "Among adult community residents, to what extent does each aspect of T1 loneliness uniquely predict depressive symptoms 6 months later, and vice versa?" Note that it says "predict", not "cause", and asks about both directions.

5. **Write down in advance "what result would change my mind":** for example, "If the loneliness → depression bridge edges do not replicate in T2 → T3, I will report them only as exploratory findings."

This lesson's `R/L10-1.R` generates a simple analysis plan, `R/output/L10-1_analysis_plan.md`, covering in order: research question, nodes, estimation method, centrality, stability, sensitivity and missing data. Fill in your own version first, settle each item, and only then start the analysis.

**Common mistakes when designing a project**

- **Overlooking ethics and data security.** Social work research often involves vulnerable groups. Even with secondary data, confirm what your permission to use it covers, and remove identifying information.

- **Cleaning data without a record.** Put every deletion and recoding step in a script and document it in the codebook (`L10-1_codebook_stats.csv` is an example).

### Self-check questions for a one-page plan

Before you start the analysis, check your plan with these five questions: ① Can the research question be stated in one sentence, without causal words? ② Why was each node chosen, and is there theory or literature behind it? ③ Is the ratio of sample size to nodes reasonable, and do you plan to check stability with the bootstrap and CS? ④ Is every setting of the main analysis (λ rule, seed, covariates) written down? ⑤ Have you decided in advance which analyses are sensitivity analyses and which are exploratory? If you can answer "yes" to all five, you are ready to start.

## ④ Concept Check Instant Check

Which research question best suits a CLPN?

- A. Does loneliness cause depression?

- It uses causal wording.

- B. Are loneliness and depression correlated?

- Too broad; cross-sectional data could answer it.

- C. To what extent do T1 loneliness items uniquely predict T2 depression items, and vice versa? (Correct)

- Correct: not causal, item level, two-directional.

- D. Does the T1 loneliness total predict the T2 depression total?

- Plausible but wrong: no causal wording, but it stays at the level of total scores and asks only one direction, so it does not use what a CLPN is good at; a CLPM could answer it.

Why write the analysis plan before seeing the results?

- A. It is a formal course requirement

- The formality is only the surface; the real purpose is to stop you from choosing the analysis after seeing the results.

- B. To limit researcher degrees of freedom and avoid choosing the analysis according to the results (Correct)

- Correct.

- C. To make sure the results match the expectations

- A plan does not guarantee results; it only guarantees that the analysis does not change with the results. If the results do not match expectations, you still report them.

- D. To make the results more significant

- Quite the opposite: the plan prevents choosing analyses in order to get significance.

On a 1–4 scale, what does an original 2 become after reverse scoring?

- A. 2

- (4+1)−2 = 3.

- B. 3 (Correct)

- Correct.

- C. 4

- That is the result for an original 1.

- D. 1

- That is the result for an original 4.

Dropouts have significantly higher baseline depression than stayers. What does this mean?

- A. No effect: the dropouts are no longer in the sample

- It is precisely because the dropouts are not in the sample that the people left are systematically "healthier".

- B. Analyzing only stayers makes the sample "healthier" and may bias the results; report it and consider imputation (Correct)

- Correct.

- C. Fill in the dropouts' missing values with the sample mean

- Mean imputation underestimates variance, distorts correlations, and ignores the information that dropping out is related to baseline depression; consider multiple imputation or FIML (Lesson 4.3).

- D. As long as retention reaches 80%, it can be ignored

- Plausible but wrong: the retention rate in this lesson's example is exactly 0.80, and the dropouts' baseline depression is still far higher than the stayers'; bias depends on how attrition relates to the variables, not only on the proportion.

Why does this course make the full procedure of Wysocki et al. (2025) a required sensitivity analysis?

- A. Because LASSO-only results are always wrong

- Not "wrong": LASSO tends to report too many edges, but the main analysis still uses it so the results can be compared with the existing literature.

- B. LASSO alone gives many false positives and pulls coefficients toward 0, and the methods paper recommends the full procedure; only with both side by side can you see which conclusions do not depend on the estimation method (Correct)

- Correct.

- C. Because the full procedure separates between-person and within-person effects

- A common misunderstanding. The full procedure is also based on observed scores and also mixes between-person and within-person effects (Lesson 7.5).

- D. Because the full procedure keeps more edges, so the results are more significant

- Quite the opposite: after pruning at p < .01, the full procedure usually keeps far fewer edges than lambda.min.

When should the rule for "how to interpret disagreement between the two analyses" be set?

- A. After seeing both results, pick the more reasonable one

- Choosing after seeing the results is a researcher degree of freedom.

- B. Before seeing any results, in the dated analysis plan (Correct)

- Correct.

- C. When a reviewer asks

- By then you have already seen the results.

- D. No need: when they disagree, always go with the main analysis

- That is a rule too, but its reasons must be written down in advance; and edges found only in the main analysis should be labeled "sensitive to the estimation method".

In the course data, each interval of the full procedure has 9 autoregressive paths and K cross-lagged paths entering the SEM. How many degrees of freedom does the nested χ² test of equality across time have?

- A. K

- The autoregressive paths count too: they are also constrained to be equal in the two intervals.

- B. 9 + K (Correct)

- Correct: the unconstrained model has 2(9 + K) path parameters and the constrained model 9 + K; the difference is 9 + K.

- C. 2(9 + K)

- That is the number of path parameters in the unconstrained model, not the difference between the two models.

- D. 72

- 72 is the number of possible cross-lagged positions.

At a minimum, a codebook should contain…

- A. Just the variable names

- With only variable names, nobody knows what lone4 asks.

- B. Variable name, item wording, scale, values and their meaning, wave (Correct)

- Correct.

- C. The mean and SD of each variable

- These are descriptive statistics and part of the codebook; without item wording and scoring, the variables still cannot be understood.

- D. Variable names and ranges are enough; the item wording goes in the paper

- Plausible but wrong: a codebook should stand on its own; if the item wording is not in it, a few months later even you will have to dig through the paper.

## Summary Key Takeaways & Further Reading

- Research question: not causal, item level, two-directional.

- Data cleaning: ranges of values, duplicate IDs, missing values, reverse scoring; the codebook records every variable.

- Attrition analysis: compare stayers and dropouts and decide how to handle missing data.

- Write a dated one-page analysis plan before seeing the results (simple preregistration).

- The main analysis is the LASSO-only CLPN; the full procedure of Wysocki et al. (2025) is a required sensitivity analysis, and both its settings and "what to do when the two analyses disagree" are written into the plan in advance.
