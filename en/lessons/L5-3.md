This lesson separates between-person questions from within-person questions and introduces the RI-CLPM, which can tell the two apart. The limitations section of a CLPN paper has to discuss this point, and reviewers often ask: "Are your results between-person or within-person?"

## ① Intuition Build the Mental Picture

### Two questions that sound alike but are completely different

- **Between-person question:** "Are people who are lonelier than others also more depressed than others?" This compares different people.

- **Within-person question:** "When a person is lonelier than they usually are, do they later become more depressed than they usually are?" This compares the same person at different times.

What social work interventions really care about is usually the second question: if we help a resident become less lonely (relative to their own usual level), will their depression improve as a result? But CLPM coefficients mix the two kinds of information.

In the demonstration below, 12 people are each measured 6 times. You can set the "between-person" and "within-person" correlations separately. By default, people with high trait loneliness also have high trait depression (ρ_B = 0.7), but for any one person, times when loneliness is higher than usual are times when depression is slightly lower (ρ_W = −0.4). Switch between the three views and see.

(Interactive widget: bw)

You will find that a correlation computed on all the points pooled together is driven mainly by "differences between people," and it can have the opposite sign from "the relationship within each person." In statistics this phenomenon has the same roots as Simpson's paradox and the ecological fallacy.

### The critique by Hamaker et al. (2015)

Hamaker, Kuiper and Grasman pointed out that many psychological characteristics have trait-like stability: some people are simply lonelier than others over long periods. The autoregressive paths of a CLPM treat these "stable individual differences" as "carryover from T1 to T2," with three consequences:

1. The autoregressive paths are overestimated (things look very stable, but it is "people being different," not "states carrying over");

2. The cross-lagged paths may be overestimated, underestimated, or even get the wrong sign, because they are mixed with "the correlation between the traits";

3. Researchers believe they have found a within-person dynamic process when much of it is actually between-person difference.

### The RI-CLPM's solution: give each person a "personal baseline"

The random-intercept CLPM (RI-CLPM) splits each observed score into two parts:

**Decomposition:**

Observed score = grand mean + the person's stable deviation (random intercept, constant over time) + this occasion's deviation from the person's baseline (within-person component, changes over time)

The autoregressive and cross-lagged paths are then placed only between the within-person components. The cross-lagged path now means: *if a person is lonelier than they usually are at T1, are they more depressed than they usually are at T2?* That is a question much closer to a "process." The correlation between the two random intercepts expresses the between-person relationship separately ("people who are lonely in the long run are also depressed in the long run").

### Comparison on the course data

The simulated data were deliberately generated with fairly strong stable traits (loneliness and depression traits correlated .50). Actual results from the companion script `R/L5-3.R`:

| Path | CLPM (standardized) | RI-CLPM within-person (standardized) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Loneliness T1 → loneliness T2 (autoregressive) | 0.607 | 0.348 |
| Depression T1 → depression T2 (autoregressive) | 0.539 | 0.218 |
| Loneliness T1 → depression T2 (cross-lagged) | 0.128 | 0.265 |
| Depression T1 → loneliness T2 (cross-lagged) | 0.016 | 0.083 |
| Loneliness T2 → loneliness T3 (autoregressive) | 0.559 | 0.198 |
| Depression T2 → loneliness T3 (cross-lagged) | 0.068 | 0.245 |
| Model comparison | χ²(4) = 67.99 | χ²(1) = 0.33; Δχ²(3) = 67.65, p < .001 |

Two observations: (1) The autoregressive paths of the RI-CLPM are much smaller: a large part of the CLPM's "stability" comes from between-person differences. (2) The cross-lagged values change too; for example, "depression T2 → loneliness T3" goes from 0.068 to 0.245. Be careful with point (2): when you compare with the large-sample values in the next section, you will see that this change is mainly sampling fluctuation in this one sample, so you cannot conclude from it that "depression → loneliness" is stronger within persons. (Note: the within-person components have smaller variances, so their standardized values cannot be compared one by one with the CLPM's, but the drop in the autoregressive paths is robust. Also, strictly speaking, the CLPM is the RI-CLPM with the random-intercept variances fixed at 0; since a variance cannot be below 0, that value lies on the boundary of the parameter space, which makes the ordinary χ² difference test conservative. Here the difference is huge (Δχ² = 67.65), so the conclusion is not affected.)

### Against large-sample values: how far is one sample from the population?

The course data are simulated, so the generating model is known. The companion script `R/L5-3b.R` fits the same two models to a sample of 200,000 people to get the standardized paths for an infinitely large sample (the "large-sample value" in the table below). The objects here are total scores, not the 9 items, so these are not the two item-level matrices used from Lesson 7.2 on: the large-sample value in a CLPM row is the CLPM's population target (trait confounding included), and the large-sample values in the RI-CLPM within-person rows correspond to the within-person process at the total-score level in the generating model. The script then generates 200 datasets of N = 800 from the same generating model, to show how far a single sample's estimate wanders around the large-sample value.

| Path (RI-CLPM within-person, standardized) | Large-sample value | Course sample | 90% range over 200 samples of N = 800 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Loneliness T1 → loneliness T2 (autoregressive) | 0.332 | 0.348 | [0.215, 0.440] |
| Loneliness T1 → depression T2 (cross-lagged) | 0.093 | 0.265 | [−0.016, 0.197] |
| Depression T1 → loneliness T2 (cross-lagged) | 0.012 | 0.083 | [−0.121, 0.093] |
| Depression T2 → loneliness T3 (cross-lagged) | 0.011 | 0.245 | [−0.095, 0.124] |
| Random-intercept correlation (between-person) | 0.459 | 0.200 | [0.325, 0.588] |

**What the table shows:**

1. **The drop in the autoregressive paths is real.** The large-sample value of the within-person autoregressive path is 0.332, while the CLPM gives 0.605 even with an infinite sample: the CLPM's "stability" really is inflated by the stable traits.

2. **The true direction is the same as in the CLPM, but the course sample overestimates both directions.** The within-person large-sample values are about 0.093 for loneliness → depression and about 0.012 for depression → loneliness, the same direction as the CLPM (loneliness → depression is stronger). The course sample's "loneliness T1 → depression T2 = 0.265" is larger than all 200 replicate estimates, and "depression T2 → loneliness T3 = 0.245" is also larger than all 200 replicate estimates; at the same time, the random-intercept correlation of 0.200 is at or above only 1 of 200 replicate samples. These numbers are off together: the random intercepts absorbed too little of the between-person association of loneliness with depression, and that part of the association moved into the within-person cross-lagged paths. The course data happen to be a rather untypical sample.

3. **With 3 waves and N = 800, the within-person cross-lagged paths of an RI-CLPM are very imprecise.** One sample's estimate can land anywhere in a range as wide as the fourth column above; if you compare the two directions by their point estimates alone, you can easily reach the opposite conclusion.

So the value of the RI-CLPM is that it removes between-person confounding, and the price is precision. Always report confidence intervals; to compare the two directions, test their difference instead of comparing two point estimates.

**What this means for CLPN:**

A CLPN (both the simplified "LASSO-only" version that is this course's main approach and the full procedure of Wysocki et al., 2025) uses observed item scores and has **no** random intercepts, so, like the CLPM, it mixes between-person and within-person information. In the course data, `data/true_B_within.csv` (the within-person truth: the within-person structure used to generate the data) and `data/true_B_population.csv` (the population target: what a CLPN converges to in an infinitely large sample) are clearly different; the latter has many extra small positive edges caused by the stable traits. From Lesson 7.2 on, the two appear side by side, and Lesson 7.5 discusses them in detail.

### Requirements and limitations of the RI-CLPM

- It needs at least 3 waves: with 2 waves, the "stable part" and the "changing part" cannot be separated (the model is not identified).

- It assumes the trait part is completely constant over time; if there are long-term trends, you may need a more complex model (e.g., adding random slopes or a linear trend; the three extensions discussed by Mulder and Hamaker (2021) add time-invariant predictors and outcomes, multiple-group comparisons, and multiple-indicator models).

- It is still only "prediction over time within persons"; it does not turn into causation automatically (Lesson 5.4).

### Common questions and pitfalls

Doesn't the CLPM already control for T1 through the autoregressive paths? Why do stable traits still leak into the cross-lagged paths?

Because the CLPM controls for the T1 **observed score**, and observed score = stable trait + this occasion's state (plus measurement error). Controlling for a score that only partly reflects the trait absorbs only part of the trait, much like "controlling" for a construct with one noisy item. The part of the trait that is not absorbed still affects both T2 loneliness and T2 depression. If the loneliness trait and the depression trait are correlated, T1 loneliness predicts T2 depression through this channel, and it shows up as a cross-lagged path. The larger the share of state in the observed score and the stronger the correlation between traits, the more leaks in. The random intercepts of the RI-CLPM model the traits separately and put the cross-lagged paths only between the within-person components, which cuts this channel (provided the traits do not change over time).

## ② Essential Math Write the Intuition as Equations

Take loneliness as an example. For person \(i\) at time \(t\), the observed score is:

\[\text{Loneliness}_{it} = \mu_t + \kappa_i + w_{it}\]

\[w^{L}_{i,t+1} = \alpha\, w^{L}_{it} + \gamma\, w^{D}_{it} + u^{L}_{i,t+1}\]

| Symbol | Reading / Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| \(i,\ t\) | Subscripts: person i, measurement t |
| \(\mu_t\) | Greek letter mu: the overall mean at wave t (the average of everyone) |
| \(\kappa_i\) | Greek letter kappa: person i's random intercept = how far they are stably above/below the mean (constant over time) |
| \(w_{it}\) | Within-person component: how far this occasion is from the person's own baseline (within) |
| \(w^{L},\ w^{D}\) | Superscript L = loneliness, D = depression: their within-person components |
| \(\alpha\) | Alpha: within-person autoregressive path (how much a lonelier-than-usual state carries over) |
| \(\gamma\) | Gamma: within-person cross-lagged path (more depressed than usual → lonelier than usual next time) |
| \(u_{i,t+1}\) | Residual of the within-person equation (a new shock, the innovation) |

#### Why is the CLPM biased? A two-line derivation

If you ignore \(\kappa_i\) and predict \(\text{Loneliness}_{t+1}\) from \(\text{Loneliness}_t\), their covariance is

\[\mathrm{Cov}(\text{Loneliness}_t,\text{Loneliness}_{t+1}) = \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}(\kappa)}_{\text{between-person}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{Cov}(w_t, w_{t+1})}_{\text{within-person}}\]

The first term has nothing to do with any process over time, yet the CLPM treats it as "stability." In the same way, the covariance of the loneliness and depression random intercepts, \(\mathrm{Cov}(\kappa^L,\kappa^D)\), leaks into the cross-lagged paths. The RI-CLPM separates these parts by estimating \(\kappa\) as its own latent variable.

**How to do it in lavaan:**

A random intercept is a latent variable whose loadings on the three waves of observed scores are all fixed at 1 (`RI_lone =~ 1*lone1 + 1*lone2 + 1*lone3`); each observed score also gets a within-person latent variable (loading 1), and the error variance of the observed variable itself is fixed at 0.

## ③ R Code Implement in R

Core of R/L5-3.R (structure follows the lavaan example by Mulder & Hamaker)

```r
riclpm <- '
  # 1) Random intercepts: each person's stable "trait level," loadings fixed at 1
  RI_lone =~ 1*lone1 + 1*lone2 + 1*lone3
  RI_dep  =~ 1*dep1  + 1*dep2  + 1*dep3
  # 2) Within-person components
  wl1 =~ 1*lone1; wl2 =~ 1*lone2; wl3 =~ 1*lone3
  wd1 =~ 1*dep1;  wd2 =~ 1*dep2;  wd3 =~ 1*dep3
  # 3) Within-person autoregressive and cross-lagged paths
  wl2 ~ wl1 + wd1;  wd2 ~ wd1 + wl1
  wl3 ~ wl2 + wd2;  wd3 ~ wd2 + wl2
  # 4) Within-wave correlations and (residual) variances
  wl1 ~~ wd1; wl2 ~~ wd2; wl3 ~~ wd3
  wl1 ~~ wl1; wd1 ~~ wd1; wl2 ~~ wl2; wd2 ~~ wd2; wl3 ~~ wl3; wd3 ~~ wd3
  RI_lone ~~ RI_lone; RI_dep ~~ RI_dep; RI_lone ~~ RI_dep
  # 5) Random intercepts uncorrelated with the T1 within-person components; observed-variable errors fixed at 0
  RI_lone ~~ 0*wl1 + 0*wd1;  RI_dep ~~ 0*wl1 + 0*wd1
  lone1 ~~ 0*lone1; lone2 ~~ 0*lone2; lone3 ~~ 0*lone3
  dep1 ~~ 0*dep1;   dep2 ~~ 0*dep2;   dep3 ~~ 0*dep3
'
fit_ri <- lavaan(riclpm, data = wide, missing = "fiml",
                 meanstructure = TRUE, int.ov.free = TRUE)
summary(fit_ri, standardized = TRUE, fit.measures = TRUE)
anova(fit_c, fit_ri)    # Compare with the CLPM (the CLPM is nested in the RI-CLPM)
```

**Block by block:** block 1 defines the two random intercepts; block 2 gives each observed score a within-person latent variable; block 3 is the within-person dynamics we care about; block 4 holds the within-wave correlations and the variances; block 5 contains the fixed constraints needed for identification. `wide` and `fit_c` come from the earlier part of the script (the same total scores and CLPM as in 5.2).

**What to look at in the results:**

(1) The standardized value of `RI_lone ~~ RI_dep` = the between-person correlation; (2) whether the random-intercept variances are clearly above 0 (if they are close to 0, the RI-CLPM and the CLPM reach similar conclusions); (3) the within-person cross-lagged paths such as `wl2 ~ wd1`; (4) the model comparison from `anova()`.

### Companion Script & Output

Complete companion script `R/L5-3.R` (46 lines, click to expand)

```r
# =====================================================================
# L5-3.R  RI-CLPM: separating stable trait differences from within-person fluctuations
# Syntax structure follows the lavaan example in Mulder & Hamaker (2021)
# =====================================================================
source("R/_setup.R")
library(lavaan)
log_open("L5-3_log.txt")
dat <- read_course_data()
wide <- data.frame(id = dat$id)
for (t in 1:3) {
  wide[[paste0("lone", t)]] <- rowSums(dat[, paste0(lone_items, "_t", t)])
  wide[[paste0("dep", t)]]  <- rowSums(dat[, paste0(dep_items, "_t", t)])
}
riclpm <- '
  # 1) Random intercepts: stable individual "trait levels", factor loadings fixed to 1
  RI_lone =~ 1*lone1 + 1*lone2 + 1*lone3
  RI_dep  =~ 1*dep1  + 1*dep2  + 1*dep3
  # 2) Within-person components
  wl1 =~ 1*lone1; wl2 =~ 1*lone2; wl3 =~ 1*lone3
  wd1 =~ 1*dep1;  wd2 =~ 1*dep2;  wd3 =~ 1*dep3
  # 3) Within-person autoregressions and cross-lags
  wl2 ~ wl1 + wd1;  wd2 ~ wd1 + wl1
  wl3 ~ wl2 + wd2;  wd3 ~ wd2 + wl2
  # 4) Synchronous covariances and (residual) variances
  wl1 ~~ wd1; wl2 ~~ wd2; wl3 ~~ wd3
  wl1 ~~ wl1; wd1 ~~ wd1; wl2 ~~ wl2; wd2 ~~ wd2; wl3 ~~ wl3; wd3 ~~ wd3
  RI_lone ~~ RI_lone; RI_dep ~~ RI_dep; RI_lone ~~ RI_dep
  # 5) Random intercepts uncorrelated with T1 within-person components; indicator measurement errors fixed to 0
  RI_lone ~~ 0*wl1 + 0*wd1;  RI_dep ~~ 0*wl1 + 0*wd1
  lone1 ~~ 0*lone1; lone2 ~~ 0*lone2; lone3 ~~ 0*lone3
  dep1 ~~ 0*dep1;   dep2 ~~ 0*dep2;   dep3 ~~ 0*dep3
'
fit_ri <- lavaan(riclpm, data = wide, missing = "fiml", meanstructure = TRUE, int.ov.free = TRUE)
print(summary(fit_ri, standardized = TRUE, fit.measures = TRUE))   # print(): ensures output is logged when running via source() or run_all.R

clpm <- 'lone2 ~ lone1 + dep1; dep2 ~ dep1 + lone1; lone3 ~ lone2 + dep2; dep3 ~ dep2 + lone2
         lone1 ~~ dep1; lone2 ~~ dep2; lone3 ~~ dep3'
fit_c <- sem(clpm, data = wide, missing = "fiml")
get <- function(f) { pe <- standardizedSolution(f); pe <- pe[pe$op == "~", ]
  setNames(round(pe$est.std, 3), paste(pe$rhs, "→", pe$lhs)) }
cat("\n===== CLPM vs. RI-CLPM Standardized Paths =====\nCLPM:\n"); print(get(fit_c))
cat("RI-CLPM (within-person):\n"); print(get(fit_ri))
print(anova(fit_c, fit_ri))
cat("Simulated data were generated with strong stable traits: RI-CLPM autoregressions drop markedly, showing that\n",
    "much of the CLPM's \"stability\" arises from between-person differences. Standard CLPN shares this limitation (Lesson 7.5).\n")
log_close()
```

Supplementary script `R/L5-3b.R` (112 lines, click to expand)

```r
# =====================================================================
# L5-3b.R  How far is one sample from the truth in RI-CLPM?
# Course data are simulated, so the data-generating model is known. This script does two things:
#   1. Fit the same CLPM and RI-CLPM to a massive sample of N = 200,000 to obtain standardized paths when sample size is infinite (truth)
#   2. Repeatedly generate 200 samples of N = 800 to see how much a single sample's estimates fluctuate around the truth
# Takes about 1–3 minutes to run. Output written to R/output/L5-3b_log.txt and L5-3b_truth.csv.
# =====================================================================
source("R/_setup.R")
library(lavaan)
log_open("L5-3b_log.txt")

# ---- Load data-generating model ------------------------------------------
# Execute only the "model definition" portion of data/simulate_data.R (before N <- 800: B, Psi, gen_panel(), to_likert()),
# without regenerating or overwriting the course data file.
ex <- parse("data/simulate_data.R")
stop_at <- which(vapply(ex, function(e) identical(deparse(e)[1], "N <- 800"), logical(1)))
stopifnot(length(stop_at) == 1)
sim <- new.env()
for (e in ex[seq_len(stop_at - 1)]) eval(e, sim)

# Sum items into 3-wave construct scores (same as L5-3.R)
to_sums <- function(X) {
  out <- list()
  for (t in 1:3) {
    lik <- sim$to_likert(X[[t]])
    out[[paste0("lone", t)]] <- rowSums(lik[, 1:4])
    out[[paste0("dep", t)]]  <- rowSums(lik[, 5:9])
  }
  as.data.frame(out)
}

riclpm <- '
  RI_lone =~ 1*lone1 + 1*lone2 + 1*lone3
  RI_dep  =~ 1*dep1  + 1*dep2  + 1*dep3
  wl1 =~ 1*lone1; wl2 =~ 1*lone2; wl3 =~ 1*lone3
  wd1 =~ 1*dep1;  wd2 =~ 1*dep2;  wd3 =~ 1*dep3
  wl2 ~ wl1 + wd1;  wd2 ~ wd1 + wl1
  wl3 ~ wl2 + wd2;  wd3 ~ wd2 + wl2
  wl1 ~~ wd1; wl2 ~~ wd2; wl3 ~~ wd3
  wl1 ~~ wl1; wd1 ~~ wd1; wl2 ~~ wl2; wd2 ~~ wd2; wl3 ~~ wl3; wd3 ~~ wd3
  RI_lone ~~ RI_lone; RI_dep ~~ RI_dep; RI_lone ~~ RI_dep
  RI_lone ~~ 0*wl1 + 0*wd1;  RI_dep ~~ 0*wl1 + 0*wd1
  lone1 ~~ 0*lone1; lone2 ~~ 0*lone2; lone3 ~~ 0*lone3
  dep1 ~~ 0*dep1;   dep2 ~~ 0*dep2;   dep3 ~~ 0*dep3
'
clpm <- 'lone2 ~ lone1 + dep1; dep2 ~ dep1 + lone1; lone3 ~ lone2 + dep2; dep3 ~ dep2 + lone2
         lone1 ~~ dep1; lone2 ~~ dep2; lone3 ~~ dep3'

# Extract standardized paths; rewrite RI-CLPM within-person variable names wl/wd to lone/dep to align with CLPM
paths <- function(fit) {
  pe <- standardizedSolution(fit)
  pe <- pe[pe$op == "~" | (pe$op == "~~" & pe$lhs == "RI_lone" & pe$rhs == "RI_dep"), ]
  nm <- ifelse(pe$op == "~", paste(pe$rhs, "→", pe$lhs), "RI_lone ~~ RI_dep")
  nm <- gsub("wl", "lone", gsub("wd", "dep", nm))
  setNames(pe$est.std, nm)
}
# Note: lavaan saves and restores random number generator state during estimation. If to_sums(gen_panel(...)) is passed directly
# as an argument, data generation occurs inside lavaan and the RNG state is subsequently restored, drawing the identical sample every time.
# Therefore, use force(d) to generate data outside lavaan first.
fit_both <- function(d, ...) {
  force(d)
  list(
    CLPM    = paths(sem(clpm, data = d, ...)),
    RICLPM  = paths(lavaan(riclpm, data = d, meanstructure = TRUE, int.ov.free = TRUE, ...)))
}

# ---- 1. Course dataset (same model as L5-3.R) -----------------------------
dat <- read_course_data()
wide <- data.frame(row.names = seq_len(nrow(dat)))
for (t in 1:3) {
  wide[[paste0("lone", t)]] <- rowSums(dat[, paste0(lone_items, "_t", t)])
  wide[[paste0("dep", t)]]  <- rowSums(dat[, paste0(dep_items, "_t", t)])
}
course <- fit_both(wide, missing = "fiml")

# ---- 2. Truth: large sample of N = 200,000 -------------------------------
set.seed(5302)
truth <- fit_both(to_sums(sim$gen_panel(200000)$X))

# ---- 3. Sampling variability: 200 samples of N = 800 ----------------------
R_REP <- 200
set.seed(5303)
reps <- lapply(seq_len(R_REP), function(r) {
  res <- tryCatch(suppressWarnings(fit_both(to_sums(sim$gen_panel(800)$X))), error = function(e) NULL)
  res
})
ok <- !vapply(reps, is.null, logical(1))
cat("Successfully converged across 200 replications:", sum(ok), "times\n\n")

rows <- list()
for (m in c("CLPM", "RICLPM")) {
  sims <- do.call(rbind, lapply(reps[ok], `[[`, m))
  for (p in names(truth[[m]])) {
    rows[[length(rows) + 1]] <- data.frame(
      model = m, path = p,
      truth = truth[[m]][[p]], course = course[[m]][[p]],
      q05 = unname(quantile(sims[, p], .05)), q95 = unname(quantile(sims[, p], .95)),
      sd = sd(sims[, p]),
      pct = mean(sims[, p] <= course[[m]][[p]]))   # Percentile rank of course sample estimate across 200 samples
  }
}
tab <- do.call(rbind, rows)
write.csv(tab, out("L5-3b_truth.csv"), row.names = FALSE)

cat("===== Standardized paths: truth (N = 200,000), course sample, and 90% range across 200 samples of N = 800 =====\n")
show <- tab; show[, 3:7] <- round(show[, 3:7], 3); show$pct <- round(tab$pct, 2)
print(show, row.names = FALSE)
cat("\nInterpretation: truth is the value when sample size is infinite; course is the estimate from course data (N = 800);\n",
    "[q05, q95] is the 90% range of estimates across 200 samples of N = 800 under the same data-generating model;\n",
    "pct is the percentile rank of the course sample estimate.\n",
    "Within-person cross-lagged paths in RI-CLPM fluctuate widely with 3 waves and N = 800: directional claims from a single sample are unreliable.\n")
log_close()
```

## ④ Concept Check Instant Check

"When a person is lonelier than they usually are, they later become more depressed than they usually are" describes:

- A. A between-person effect

- Between-person compares different people; the reference point in this sentence is the person themselves.

- B. A within-person effect (Correct)

- Correct: the reference point is "their own usual level."

- C. A within-wave correlation

- A within-wave correlation describes change together at the same moment and says nothing about "later."

- D. Measurement error

- Measurement error is random noise in the scores, not a relationship about "later."

What does the random intercept in an RI-CLPM stand for?

- A. The overall mean at each wave

- The overall mean at each wave is μ_t, shared by everyone; the random intercept is each person's own stable deviation.

- B. Each person's stable individual difference that does not change over time (the trait-like part) (Correct)

- Correct.

- C. The cross-lagged coefficients

- The cross-lagged paths sit between the within-person components, not on the random intercepts.

- D. Measurement error

- In this model the observed errors are fixed at 0; the random intercept is the part that is stable over time, not error.

Why does the RI-CLPM need at least 3 waves?

- A. A software limitation

- It is a statistical identification problem; any software has it.

- B. With 2 waves the stable part and the changing part cannot be separated (not identified) (Correct)

- Correct.

- C. Because estimating cross-lagged paths needs at least two time intervals

- Two waves give one interval, and a CLPM can still estimate cross-lagged paths; the RI-CLPM needs 3 waves to separate the stable part from the changing part.

- D. Because equality constraints have to be estimated

- Equality constraints are optional; without them, a 2-wave RI-CLPM is still not identified.

In the course data, the autoregressive coefficients drop clearly when you switch from the CLPM to the RI-CLPM. What is the most reasonable explanation?

- A. The RI-CLPM fits worse, so the coefficients are pushed down

- The RI-CLPM fits clearly better (Δχ²(3) = 67.65); the coefficients shrink because the random intercepts take over the stable individual differences.

- B. Much of the CLPM's "stability" comes from stable between-person differences (Correct)

- Correct.

- C. Within-person states actually have no inertia

- The within-person autoregressive paths are still positive (e.g., 0.348), just smaller than in the CLPM: there is inertia, but less than the CLPM shows.

- D. The within-person process is more stable

- The opposite: smaller within-person autoregressive paths mean that states carry over less than the CLPM suggests.

Does a standard CLPN already separate between-person from within-person?

- A. Yes, a CLPN is a within-person model by nature

- A common misunderstanding: a CLPN uses observed scores and has no random intercepts.

- B. No; it is based on observed scores and, like the CLPM, mixes the two, which you must say when you interpret results and write the limitations (Correct)

- Correct: this is a point every CLPN paper must make in its limitations.

- C. Only when lambda.1se is used

- λ only decides how many edges are shrunk away; it does not separate the stable traits from the scores.

- D. Standardizing is enough to separate them

- Standardizing only changes units; it cannot remove stable traits.

#### Exercise 1: One between-person and one within-person exampleBasic

Using "social participation" and "loneliness," write one between-person and one within-person research question, and imagine a situation in which the two relationships go in opposite directions.

Between-person: "Are residents who participate more socially less lonely than residents who participate less?" (probably a negative correlation). Within-person: "When a resident takes part in more activities than usual for a while, are they lonelier than usual?" #### Exercise 2: Read the table and explain it in 200 wordsWriting

Using the CLPM vs. RI-CLPM table above, write an explanation of about 200 words for "a social work colleague who has never studied statistics."

Show Reference Solution / Walkthrough

Key points: ① The ordinary model (CLPM) shows loneliness and depression as "very stable," with loneliness mainly predicting depression. ② But in these data, some people are lonelier and more depressed than others over the long run, and the ordinary model mistakes these "long-term differences" for a process of change. ③ Once each person's long-term level is taken out (RI-CLPM), we see the relationship between fluctuations of the kind "lonelier / more depressed than usual." In this sample both directions are estimated as fairly large, but the comparison with the large-sample values (the section "Against large-sample values" on this page) shows that, with 3 waves and N = 800, these within-person cross-lagged estimates vary a lot, so you cannot tell from them which direction is stronger. ④ So if an intervention aims to change a person's state, look at the results with the long-term differences removed, while admitting that such estimates are imprecise and are still statistical prediction, not causation.

#### Exercise 3: Hands-on: random-intercept variancesHands-on

Run `R/L5-3.R`, find the standardized estimates of `RI_lone ~~ RI_lone`, `RI_dep ~~ RI_dep` and `RI_lone ~~ RI_dep` in `summary(fit_ri)`, and answer: how large is the between-person correlation? Is it close to the 0.50 set in the simulation?

Show Reference Solution / Walkthrough

In the `Covariances:` part, look up the `Std.all` of `RI_lone ~~ RI_dep`: that is the correlation of the two random intercepts. The simulation set the trait correlation to 0.50, but the observed scores were built from "trait + item-specific part + within-person state" and then cut into Likert categories, so the estimate need not equal 0.50. The actual result in this data (`R/solutions/L5-3_solution.R`) is a random-intercept correlation of 0.20, 95% CI [−0.02, 0.42], clearly below 0.50 and with a very wide interval. The main reason is sampling error: under the same generating model, the random-intercept correlation for an infinite sample is 0.459 (`R/L5-3b.R`); the 90% range over 200 samples of N = 800 is [0.325, 0.588], and the course sample's 0.200 is at or above only 1 of 200 replicate samples. With only 3 waves the RI-CLPM is just identified, and the "stable part" and the "within-person part" are hard to tell apart, so the estimate is very imprecise. The large-sample value itself is also a little below 0.50; that is a secondary reason: each item also has its own independent stable component, women have a stable offset on the depression items, and Likert categories weaken correlations. Also, the random-intercept variances (loneliness 3.40, depression 4.35) are about 38% of the variance of the observed totals (the average of the per-wave variances: loneliness 9.07, depression 11.52). That is smaller than the depression ICC from the multilevel model in Lesson 4.1 (0.53): the multilevel model attributes all of the covariance across waves to stable differences between people, while the RI-CLPM attributes part of it to within-person deviations carrying over (see the paragraph "Why does Lesson 5.3 say 'the stable part is about 38%'?" in Lesson 4.1). With only 3 waves these two sources are hard to separate, so 0.38 is as imprecise as the random-intercept correlation. Even when the model's "structure is right," with 3 waves and N = 800 the between-person estimates can be far from the large-sample values, so report them with confidence intervals.

## Summary Key Takeaways & Further Reading

- Between-person: comparing people with each other; within-person: comparing a person with their own usual level. The two can go in opposite directions.

- Hamaker et al. (2015): the CLPM mixes stable traits into the autoregressive and cross-lagged paths.

- RI-CLPM = random intercepts (the stable part) + within-person components (the dynamic part); needs at least 3 waves.

- Course data: the RI-CLPM's autoregressive paths drop sharply and the model fits significantly better; with 3 waves and N = 800 the within-person cross-lagged paths are very imprecise, and a single sample cannot tell you the direction (large-sample values in L5-3b.R).

- A standard CLPN has no random intercepts; say so when you interpret the results and write the limitations.
