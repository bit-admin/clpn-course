Introduction to Logistic Regression: Binary Outcomes

Study time 1 hr 30 minPrerequisites: 3.2 Multiple Regression and 'Controlling for Other Variables'R script: R/L3-4.R

Learning Objectives

  1. Understand why ordinary linear regression is not used when the outcome is binary (e.g. 'dropped out of school or not')
  2. Read odds and odds ratios (OR)
  3. Know that glmnet can also fit logistic regression (family = "binomial"), in preparation for binary nodes

Core Concepts

  • logistic regression
  • probability
  • odds
  • log-odds / logit
  • odds ratio, OR
  • generalized linear model, GLM

① IntuitionWhen the Outcome Is Only "Yes / No"

Who comes to seek professional help?

A community service center wants to know: are residents with higher depression scores more likely to seek professional help on their own? Do men and women differ? The course data have a variable help_seek_t2: whether the person had sought professional help by T2 (1 = yes, 0 = no). Of the 800 people, 182 (22.8%) sought help: 28.6% of women and 16.1% of men. The outcome is no longer a continuous score, just "yes / no." This is called a binary outcome.

Why not just use a straight line?

If you predict a 0/1 variable with ordinary linear regression (a "linear probability model"), the predictions can be read as "the probability of seeking help." But there are three problems:

  1. Probabilities have limits; straight lines don't. A probability must lie between 0 and 1, while a line runs on forever in both directions: with a high enough depression score the predicted probability goes above 1, with a low enough one below 0, and "a −10% probability of seeking help" means nothing. (In the course data the range of depression scores is limited, and the linear model's predictions happen to fall between 0.08 and 0.55, but with another sample or with extrapolation the problem appears.)
  2. The relationship is probably not a straight line. When the probability is very low or very high, the same change usually moves the probability less: for someone who was very unlikely to seek help, 1 more point of depression won't suddenly make it likely; for someone who was already likely to, the probability can't rise much further. The change is fastest in the middle. This shape is an S.
  3. The errors break the assumptions of linear regression. The residuals of a 0/1 outcome can take only two values, their variance changes with the predicted value, and the standard errors are off.

Logistic regression gets around this: instead of fitting a line to the probability directly, it first converts the probability into a quantity without limits (the log-odds), fits a line to that, and converts back. The result is an S-shaped curve that always stays between 0 and 1.

(The points in the widget are made-up illustration data, with "loneliness" from 0 to 10 on the horizontal axis; the real results from the course data are in the R code section.)

Three conversions: probability → odds → log-odds

The odds = the probability that something happens ÷ the probability that it doesn't. Saying "the odds are 3 to 1" is an everyday example.

A probability ranges from 0 to 1; odds range from 0 to +∞; after taking the natural log, the log-odds (logit) range from −∞ to +∞. There are no limits any more, so a straight line can model them.

The odds ratio: "translating" a logistic regression coefficient

A logistic regression coefficient b means: each 1-unit increase in x raises the log-odds by b. Log-odds are hard to grasp, so you usually exponentiate, \(e^{b}\), to get the odds ratio (OR): each 1-unit increase in x multiplies the odds by the OR.

  • OR = 1: the odds don't change (no association).
  • OR = 1.5: the odds are multiplied by 1.5, i.e., 50% higher.
  • OR = 0.67: the odds are multiplied by 0.67, i.e., about 33% lower (0.67 ≈ 1/1.5: the same strength as OR = 1.5, in the opposite direction).
  • OR = 2: the odds double, but the probability does not double.
OR = 2 is not "twice the probability"

Suppose someone's probability of seeking help is 0.5 (odds = 1). OR = 2 makes the odds 2, and the probability becomes 2/(1+2) = 0.667, up only 0.167, nowhere near 1.0. Only when an event is rare (the probability is small, say < 0.1) are odds ≈ probability, so that the OR approximates a "multiple of the probability" (the risk ratio). News stories often describe an OR as "X times the risk," which exaggerates the effect when the event is not rare.

Binary outcomes in social work

Dropping out or not, accepting a referral or not, relapsing or not, joining a group or not, self-harming or not: many key outcomes in social work research are binary. Logistic regression is one of the most common tools for analyzing them.

Generalized linear models: one big family

Linear regression and logistic regression both belong to the generalized linear models (GLM): the predictors are first combined linearly, \(b_0+b_1x_1+\cdots\), and then linked to the mean of the outcome through a "link function." For linear regression the link "does nothing" (identity); for logistic regression it is the logit; for count outcomes (such as the number of times someone sought help) Poisson regression (log link) is common. R fits all of them with the same function, glm(), and you pick the family with the family argument.

The link to CLPN: binary nodes

Each CLPN equation "predicts one T2 node from all T1 nodes." If a node is a yes/no item (for example, "any suicidal thoughts in the past two weeks?"), that equation cannot use linear regression; it needs logistic regression. The glmnet package that CLPN uses also supports logistic regression with a LASSO penalty: just set family = "binomial" (there is an example in the R code section). For networks with nodes of mixed types there is also the mgm package (not covered in this course; just know the tool exists).

② Essential MathThe Logit and the S-Shaped Curve

$$\ln\!\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) = b_0 + b_1x_1 + b_2x_2$$
\(p\)
The probability that the event happens (such as seeking help = 1).
\(\frac{p}{1-p}\)
The odds.
\(\ln\)
The natural logarithm (base \(e\approx2.718\)).
\(b_0, b_1, b_2\)
Coefficients: each 1-unit increase in \(x_1\) raises the log-odds by \(b_1\).

Solving the equation for \(p\) gives the probability itself (the logistic function, S-shaped):

$$p = \frac{1}{1+e^{-(b_0+b_1x_1+b_2x_2)}}$$

However large or small the number in the parentheses, \(p\) stays between 0 and 1: as the parentheses → +∞, \(e^{-\cdots}\to0\) and \(p\to1\); as they → −∞, \(p\to0\).

Why is \(e^{b_1}\) the odds ratio?

When \(x_1\) goes from \(x\) to \(x+1\) (everything else fixed), the log-odds rise by \(b_1\): \(\ln(\text{odds}_{x+1}) - \ln(\text{odds}_x) = b_1\). A difference of logs is the log of a ratio, so \(\text{odds}_{x+1}/\text{odds}_x = e^{b_1}\). This ratio does not depend on where \(x\) starts: the OR is a fixed "multiplicative" effect, while the change in probability depends on the starting point.

The estimation method is not least squares but maximum likelihood: find the coefficients that make "observing this particular set of 0s and 1s" most likely (the models in Module 5 use the same idea).

③ R Codeglm(family = binomial)

Script for this lesson: R/L3-4.R. Gender is coded 0 = male, 1 = female.

OR <- exp(cbind(OR = coef(fit), confint.default(fit)))   # exp(coefficient) = odds ratio

Reading the output item by item
  • Estimate is the coefficient on the log-odds scale: each 1 point higher on depression raises the log-odds by 0.134.
  • z value / Pr(>|z|): logistic regression uses a z test (Wald test), read like a t test.
  • The OR table: depression OR = 1.143, 95% CI [1.089, 1.200]. Controlling for gender, each 1 point higher on depression multiplies the odds of seeking help by 1.14 (about 14% higher); the interval excludes 1, so it is significant. Gender OR = 1.887: at the same depression score, women's odds of seeking help are about 1.89 times men's. The intercept's OR of 0.116 is the odds for "a man with a depression score of 0" (a probability of about 0.104).
  • The deviance is like a "residual sum of squares": 857.98 for the intercept-only model, down to 810.16 with the two predictors. AIC is for comparing models; smaller is better. Logistic regression has no ordinary R².
  • Note: the OR's confidence interval uses confint.default() (the Wald method), \(e^{b\pm1.96SE}\); it is symmetric on the log-odds scale and becomes asymmetric on the OR scale after exponentiating.
Checking against the "truth": the advantage of simulated data

Open data/simulate_data.R and you will see how help_seek_t2 was generated: rbinom(N, 1, plogis(-2.2 + 0.18 * dep_t2_sum + 0.4 * wide$gender)). So the true depression coefficient is 0.18 (OR = e0.18 ≈ 1.20) and the true gender coefficient is 0.4 (OR ≈ 1.49). Our estimates are 0.134 and 0.635, both some way off the true values, but the 95% confidence intervals (depression about [0.086, 0.182], gender about [0.28, 0.99]) both cover them. This is what sampling error looks like (Lesson 1.5): even with a completely correct model and N = 800, a single estimate will not land exactly on the true value; a binary outcome carries less information than a continuous score, so the error is larger. plogis() is R's logistic function \(1/(1+e^{-x})\), and qlogis() is its inverse, the logit.

Worked example: from logit to probability

Suppose the model is logit(p) = −2 + 0.5x, where x is the depression total and p the probability of "seeking help."

  1. x = 2: logit = −1, p = 1/(1 + e1) ≈ 0.269.
  2. x = 3: logit = −0.5, p ≈ 0.378.
  3. x = 4: logit = 0, p = 0.5.
  4. OR = e0.5 ≈ 1.65. From x = 2 to 3, the odds go from 0.269/0.731 ≈ 0.368 to 0.368 × 1.65 ≈ 0.607, which is p = 0.607/1.607 ≈ 0.378, the same as step 2.

Note how the probability grows: from 2 to 3 it rises by about 0.11, from 3 to 4 by about 0.12, and toward the two ends of the curve the increases get smaller and smaller. The OR is fixed; the change in probability is not.

Common mistakes
  • Reading the Estimate column directly. summary(glm) gives log-odds; you need exp() to get ORs. Exponentiate the confidence interval as well: this lesson always uses exp(confint.default(fit)) (the Wald interval, \(e^{b\pm1.96SE}\)). For a glm, confint(fit) gives a different interval, the profile likelihood interval, which is more accurate than the Wald interval with small samples or few events; with a larger sample and not too few events, the two are usually very close. Either is fine, but use one consistently within a report and say which in the methods section.
  • Ignoring a "complete separation" warning. If a predictor separates the 0s and 1s perfectly, R warns "fitted probabilities numerically 0 or 1 occurred," and the coefficients and SEs become huge. Then check the data instead of copying the results.
  • Putting many predictors into a model with few events. A common rule of thumb is at least 10 events in the smaller class per predictor.

A reporting template

A logistic regression in a paper usually reports: the outcome and its coding (1 = sought help, 0 = did not), the number of people in each group, and for each predictor its OR with 95% CI and p-value. For example: "Controlling for gender, each 1 point higher on T2 depression went with X% higher odds of seeking help (OR = …, 95% CI […, …], p = …)," where X% = (OR − 1) × 100%. If OR < 1, write "the odds were (1 − OR) × 100% lower." To give readers a feel for the effect, you can also report predicted probabilities for two typical people (for example, depression scores of 2 and 10), obtained with predict(fit, newdata, type = "response").

④ Concept CheckQuick Quiz

Why can't a probability be modeled with a straight line directly?

  • A 0/1 variable does have a mean: the proportion of "yes." The problem is that a line crosses the 0–1 limits and has the wrong shape.
  • Correct.
  • In the course data the linear probability model's predictions happen to lie between 0.08 and 0.55; the problem is that with another sample or extrapolation they go out of bounds, and the real relationship is S-shaped.
The logit transformation turns a bounded probability into unbounded log-odds, which are then modeled with a line.

When the probability is p = 0.8, what are the odds?

odds = p / (1 − p).
0.8 / 0.2 = 4, that is, "4 : 1."

Does OR = 2 mean "twice the probability"?

  • This is the misreading the lesson warns about: an OR is a ratio of odds; at a probability of 0.5, OR = 2 raises the probability only to 0.667.
  • Correct: for example, a probability of 0.5 → 0.667.
  • The confidence interval says whether the association is significant; it doesn't change what an OR is, which is always a ratio of odds.
A probability of 0.5 is odds of 1; doubling the odds gives 2, which is a probability of 2/3.

A factor has OR = 0.67. What is the best interpretation?

  • Two mistakes: it is the odds that change, not the probability, and multiplying by 0.67 is about 33% lower, not 67%.
  • Multiplying by 0.67 keeps 67% and lowers the odds by about 33%.
  • Correct.
  • No association is OR = 1; 0.67 means a negative association.
0.67 ≈ 1/1.5: the same size as OR = 1.5, in the opposite direction.

In the course data, depression has b = 0.134. Compute OR = e^b (two decimal places).

e^0.134 ≈ ?
e^0.134 ≈ 1.143: each 1 point higher on depression raises the odds of seeking help by about 14%.

Your network has a yes/no item (such as "any self-harm"). What should the equation that has it as the outcome use?

  • A straight line for a binary outcome crosses the 0–1 limits, and the errors break the assumptions of linear regression.
  • Correct.

⑤ ExercisesHands-On Practice

Exercise 3.4-A: Interpreting an OR

Use glm(family = binomial) to predict "sought professional help" (help_seek_t2) from the T2 depression total and gender. (1) Explain the gender OR in one sentence; (2) explain what OR = 1.5 and OR = 0.67 mean.

Reference answer

(1) Controlling for T2 depression, women's odds of seeking professional help were about 1.89 times men's, OR = 1.89, 95% CI [1.32, 2.69], p < .001. (Say "odds," not "probability" or "1.89 times as likely.")

(2) OR = 1.5: each 1-unit increase in the predictor multiplies the odds by 1.5, a 50% increase; OR = 0.67: the odds are multiplied by 0.67, about 33% lower. The two are reciprocals: associations of the same strength in opposite directions.

Exercise 3.4-B: Adding loneliness

Add the T1 loneliness total to the model. What is the OR for loneliness? Is it significant? Use predict(..., type = "response") to compare the predicted probabilities of two women with loneliness scores of 6 and 12 (both with a T2 depression score of 5).

Reference code and hints 
dat$lone_sum_t1 <- rowSums(dat[, paste0(lone_items, "_t1")])
fit2 <- glm(help_seek_t2 ~ dep_sum_t2 + gender + lone_sum_t1, data = dat, family = binomial)
round(exp(cbind(OR = coef(fit2), confint.default(fit2))), 3)
predict(fit2, newdata = data.frame(dep_sum_t2 = 5, gender = 1, lone_sum_t1 = c(6, 12)), type = "response")

Result: loneliness OR = 1.018, 95% CI [0.960, 1.078], p = .554, not significant; the ORs for depression and gender barely change (1.137, 1.904). The two women's predicted probabilities are 0.289 and 0.311, only about 2 percentage points apart. Explanation: in the simulated data, help-seeking was generated from depression and gender only (see data/simulate_data.R), so once they are controlled for, loneliness adds almost no predictive power. You know the "truth," so you can check whether the method gives the right answer. In real data, "not significant" cannot be read as "no relationship" (Lesson 3.3).

Exercise 3.4-C: Converting between probability and odds

Convert the probabilities 0.05, 0.25, and 0.6 to odds; then convert the odds 0.5 and 4 back to probabilities.

Reference answer

0.05 → 0.0526; 0.25 → 0.333; 0.6 → 1.5. Odds 0.5 → 0.5/1.5 = 0.333; odds 4 → 4/5 = 0.8. Note that at a probability of 0.05 the odds, 0.0526, are almost equal to the probability, so for rare events OR ≈ risk ratio.

Exercise 3.4-D: Straight line vs. S-curve; the binary version of glmnet

(1) Fit a linear probability model with lm(help_seek_t2 ~ dep_sum_t2 + gender, data = dat) and look at the range of the predictions with range(fitted(...)). The predictions all lie within 0–1 this time, so why is logistic regression still recommended? (2) Run this lesson's "logistic regression with LASSO" code, then change s = "lambda.min" to s = "lambda.1se". How many items have non-zero coefficients in each case?

Reference answer

(1) The predictions lie between about 0.08 and 0.55 and happen not to cross the limits. But with another sample, or extrapolating to higher depression scores, the line could give "probabilities" above 1; the real relationship is S-shaped, and the residual variance of a 0/1 outcome changes with the predicted value, so the standard errors are off. Logistic regression has none of these problems. (2) lambda.min keeps 3 depression items (dep1, dep3, dep5); with lambda.1se all 9 items are 0. Just by setting family = "binomial", the same glmnet handles a binary node.

SummaryThree Takeaways

  • Binary outcomes call for logistic regression: a straight-line model for the log-odds, so the probability follows an S-shaped curve that always stays between 0 and 1.
  • Exponentiating a coefficient gives the OR: each 1-unit increase in x multiplies the odds by the OR; an OR is not a multiple of the probability (the difference is large when the event is not rare).
  • glm is a family of models; glmnet also takes family = "binomial", which prepares you for binary nodes in a CLPN. Next lesson: 3.5 Overfitting and Regularization: Ridge, LASSO, and Cross-Validation.
