## ① Intuition Build the Mental Picture

### Why "structured reading"?

The first time you read a methods paper, it is easy to drown in terms and numbers and come away remembering only that it "looked impressive". Structured reading means reading with a fixed checklist and putting each piece of information into its slot as you find it; when you finish, the empty slots show what the paper did not report (or what you did not understand). The items on the checklist follow the content of M4–M8 in order.

### A recommended reading order

1. **Abstract** (5 minutes): research question, sample, main findings. First write a one-sentence summary of what you think the authors want to say.

2. **Figures and tables** (10 minutes): network plot, centrality plot, stability plot. Read them with what you learned in Lessons 7.3 and 8.2–8.3: which edges are thickest? Are autoregressive edges drawn? Does the centrality come with a CS-coefficient?

3. **Method section** (20 minutes): fill in the checklist item by item: sample—waves—nodes—missing data—estimation—λ—covariates—centrality—stability.

4. **Results and discussion** (20 minutes): tell apart three kinds of content (see below) and mark the sentences you think go too far.

5. **Supplementary materials and open data & code** (10 minutes): many key details (the full edge table, the λ rule, stability plots) appear only in the supplement. Check whether there is an OSF link and whether the code can be rerun.

### Three kinds of content: finding, interpretation, overreach

| Type | How to recognize it | Example (made up) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Finding | You can find a matching number in a results table or figure | "The cross-lagged coefficient from T1 loneliness item X to T2 depression item Y was 0.12." |
| Interpretation | The authors' guess at what a finding means, usually with "may" or "suggests" | "This may reflect the role of social withdrawal in the development of depression." |
| Overreach | Goes beyond what the data can support: causal words, intervention advice, generalization | "Interventions should therefore target X first to prevent depression." |

The next lesson (9.2) lists the most common kinds of overreach systematically.

### Common questions and pitfalls

A paper reports no CS-coefficient and does not share its data. How should I read its centrality conclusions?

Only as results whose stability was never checked. You can make a few quick judgments: the number of nodes and N/p (this lesson's "minimal math"), whether the intervals of the strongest edges are very wide, and how far the top node is ahead of the second. These only point to risk; they cannot replace CS. In a literature review, flag the centrality conclusions of such studies separately; as a reviewer, ask the authors to run the case-dropping bootstrap and report CS.

## ② Essential Math Write the Intuition as Equations

The "math" in this lesson is a few quick checks you will use most often when reading papers, to see whether a paper's numbers are consistent.

#### 1. Counting edges

\[\text{max. cross-lagged edges}=p(p-1),\qquad \text{density}=\frac{\#\{\hat\beta_{kj}\neq0,\ k\neq j\}}{p(p-1)}\]

If a paper says "16 nodes, 300 cross-lagged edges estimated", you know at once that this is impossible (at most 16 × 15 = 240).

#### 2. Ratio of sample size to parameters

\[\frac{N}{p}\quad(\text{people per predictor in each regression})\]

| Symbol | Reading / Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| \(N\) | Sample size used in the analysis (note: may be smaller than the number recruited because of missing data) |
| \(p\) | Number of nodes = number of predictors in each nodewise regression |

There is no accepted hard rule of the kind "N must be at least so many times p". N/p is only a rough number for a quick check while reading: the smaller it is, the less information each nodewise regression has, the larger the λ chosen by cross-validation, the sparser the network and the worse the stability. The course data have N/p = 800/9 ≈ 89; a study with N = 300 and p = 20 has N/p = 15, and its results need extra caution. How large a sample a study really needs depends on what the true network looks like (how strong the edges are, how many there are) and on the question the study asks, and has to be settled by simulation: Constantin, Schuurman and Vermunt (2026) give a general Monte Carlo method for sample size planning in network models, and Lesson 7.6 does a simplified version. So when you read a paper, a small N/p is only a prompt: check whether the authors planned their sample size and whether they report bootstrap intervals and CS-coefficients.

#### 3. Attrition rate

\[\text{attrition rate}=1-\frac{N_{\text{completed all waves}}}{N_{\text{T1}}}\]

When attrition is high (e.g., > 20%) and not dealt with, ask: is dropping out related to baseline symptoms (review Lesson 4.3)?

#### 4. Reading CS and intervals

When you see a CS, compare it at once with the 0.25 / 0.5 lines; when you see bootstrap intervals for edges, check at once whether the intervals of the strongest edges overlap and whether they contain 0.

## ③ R Code Implement in R

This lesson is mostly reading and has no R script of its own. But there is one very practical R skill: **reproducing the numbers in a paper**. If the authors share their data and code (OSF), you can check their λ rule, seed and CS-coefficients. Below is a "reproduction check" template, assuming you have downloaded the authors' wide-format data:

Reproduction check template (uses the course's clpn_estimate(); adjust variable names and rule to the paper)

```r
source("R/_setup.R")
paper <- read.csv("paper_data.csv")             # data shared by the authors (example file name)
t1 <- grep("_t1$", names(paper), value = TRUE)  # find T1 items using the authors' naming convention
t2 <- sub("_t1$", "_t2", t1)                    # the matching T2 items (same order!)
stopifnot(all(t2 %in% names(paper)))
d  <- na.omit(paper[, c(t1, t2)])               # if the authors used listwise deletion
nrow(d)                                         # does it match the N reported in the paper?
set.seed(2026)                                  # if the authors report a seed, use theirs
B  <- clpn_estimate(d[, t1], d[, t2], rule = "lambda.min")   # or the rule the authors used
CL <- cl_only(B)
sum(CL != 0)                                    # compare with the number of edges in the paper
head(edge_list(CL), 10)                         # compare with the paper's table of strongest edges
sort(rowSums(CL), decreasing = TRUE)            # out-EI ranking
```

**What if you cannot reproduce it?**

Check 4 things first: (1) is the sample the same (missing-data handling)? (2) were the variables standardized? (3) the λ rule and number of folds; (4) the random seed. Even if all of these match, different glmnet or R versions can cause small differences, which is why Lesson 9.3 asks you to report software versions.

### Worked example: labeling every sentence of an abstract

Below is a **made-up** abstract of a CLPN paper. For each sentence, decide whether it is a "finding" (directly supported by the data), an "interpretation" (reasonable but resting on assumptions) or an "overreach" (not supported by the data):

1. "This study followed 612 adult community residents over three waves, 6 months apart." (**Description**: design information. Check N, the interval and the attrition rate first.)

2. "Controlling for all T1 symptoms, T1 'Lack of companionship' positively predicted T2 'Depressed mood'." (**Finding**: provided this edge is really in the table, ideally with a bootstrap interval.)

3. "The social-deficit side of loneliness may be an early signal of worsening depression." (**Interpretation**: the "may" is appropriate, but it rests on the assumption that the predictive relationship reflects some within-person process.)

4. "'Lack of companionship' is the driving symptom of the whole network." (**Overreach**: "driving" is causal language. Even if its out-EI is the highest, you still have to check whether the CS meets the standard.)

5. "Community interventions should therefore give priority to residents' need for companionship, which will effectively prevent depression." (**Overreach**: it jumps straight from observational data to the effect of an intervention. A sound version would be "this suggests testing … in an intervention study".)

After labeling, go back to the body of the paper and check: is the edge in sentence 2 actually in the table? How wide is its interval? What is the CS behind sentence 4? In many papers the problem is not a wrong analysis, but an abstract and discussion that claim more than the results show.

**Common mistakes when reading CLPN papers**

- **Reading only the abstract and the network plot.** Edge-weight tables, stability results and sensitivity analyses are usually in the supplementary material, and they are exactly what you need to judge whether the conclusions hold.

- **Assuming the authors' λ rule, covariates and choice of nodes were the only reasonable ones.** Ask as you read: "With another reasonable choice, would the conclusion still hold?" Did the authors run sensitivity analyses?

- **Comparing edge weights across papers directly.** The interval (2 weeks or 1 year), the regularization method and the samples differ, so edge sizes are not comparable; only patterns can be compared.

- **Ignoring who the sample is.** Whether findings from a college student sample apply to adult community residents or to an agency's clients needs its own argument.

Keep a reading card for every paper you read, noting the design, nodes, estimation method, stability checks and what you see as the main problems. By the fifth paper you will start to notice the practices and problems that keep recurring in the field; they are the raw material for a literature review.

## ④ Concept Check Instant Check

Which of these is a "finding" rather than an "interpretation"?

- A. This may reflect the role of social withdrawal

- This is interpretation: "may reflect …" is the authors' guess at what a number means; you will not find this sentence in a results table.

- B. The cross-lagged coefficient from T1 item A to T2 item B was 0.12 (Correct)

- Correct: you can find the matching number in a results table.

- C. A should therefore be the first target of intervention

- This is overreach: it jumps straight from a predictive relationship to intervention advice.

- D. A is the core driver of the network

- This is overreach: "core driver" carries a causal meaning, and nothing is said about the stability of the centrality.

A paper's 16-node CLPN reports 280 cross-lagged edges. What should you do?

- A. Accept it: with a large sample there can be many edges

- The maximum number of edges depends only on the number of nodes: at most p(p − 1), whatever the sample size.

- B. Question it: there can be at most 16 × 15 = 240 (Correct)

- Correct.

- C. Accept it: the authors may have counted the autoregressive edges as well

- Even adding the 16 autoregressive edges gives only 256, still short of 280.

- D. Accept it: lambda.min keeps many edges anyway

- The λ rule only decides how many are kept; the maximum is still 240.

Why must you look at the supplementary materials when reading a CLPN paper?

- A. Results in the supplement are more reliable than in the main text

- Both are results of the same study and equally credible; the difference is the level of detail.

- B. Key information such as the full edge table, the λ rule and the stability plots is often only in the supplement (Correct)

- Correct.

- C. Journals require readers to read the supplement

- Journals have no such requirement; you read it because the information needed to judge the conclusions is often only there.

- D. The supplement contains negative results the authors hid in the main text

- Most supplements just hold details that did not fit in the main text; that is not hiding anything. But do check that the conclusions in the main text agree with the numbers in the supplement.

A paper reports a CS of 0.18 for out-EI, yet calls a node the "core driving symptom" in the discussion. This is…

- A. A reasonable interpretation: its out-EI really is the highest in the sample

- CS < 0.25 means this "highest" may change after dropping a few cases; and "driving" implies causation.

- B. Overreach: with CS < 0.25 the out-EI ranking should not be interpreted, and "driving" implies causation (Correct)

- Correct.

- C. A finding: its out-EI value is in the table

- The table contains only the out-EI value; "core driving symptom" is the authors' reading, which goes beyond the number itself.

- D. A replication result

- This has nothing to do with replication: replication means getting similar results in another interval or sample.

When reproducing someone else's CLPN results, which of these least needs checking?

- A. Missing-data handling and sample size

- Needs checking: different missing-data handling means different people enter the analysis, and the network changes.

- B. The λ rule and the random seed

- Needs checking: the rule decides how many edges are kept, the seed decides the cross-validation folds, and both change weak edges.

- C. Whether the items were standardized

- Needs checking: standardizing changes the relative strength of the LASSO penalty and therefore which edges are kept.

- D. Whether the authors used RStudio or another code editor (Correct)

- Correct: the editor is just an interface for writing code and does not affect the computation; what you need to check are the versions of R and the packages.

#### Exercise 2: Consistency checkBasics

A paper: N = 450, p = 12 nodes, reports "75 nonzero cross-lagged edges, density 0.57". Check whether the density is right and compute N/p.

Show Reference Solution / Walkthrough

At most 12 × 11 = 132 edges, and 75/132 ≈ 0.57, so the density checks out. N/p = 37.5, less than half that of the course data (≈ 89). There is no hard standard for N/p, but this value is a reminder to look closely at the bootstrap intervals and CS-coefficients the authors report, and at whether they planned their sample size.

## Summary Key Takeaways & Further Reading

- Structured reading: read with a checklist; an empty slot = not reported, or not understood.

- Order: abstract → figures and tables → methods → results and discussion → supplement and code.

- Tell apart findings, interpretation and overreach.

- Quick checks: maximum edges p(p−1), N/p, attrition rate, CS thresholds.

- Funkhouser et al. (2021): ABCD study, 4,093 adolescents, with a T2→T3 replication; take the details from the original paper.
