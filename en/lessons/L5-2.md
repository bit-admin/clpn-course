## ① Intuition Build the Mental Picture

### Why not just keep using lm()?

Four `lm()` calls also give you the 8 paths of a three-wave CLPM, but with three drawbacks: (1) they cannot estimate the residual correlations at the same time; (2) they cannot test whether "the model as a whole agrees with the data"; (3) they make it awkward to add constraints (e.g., "the paths are equal in both intervals") or to handle missing data. SEM estimates all the equations together and fits a "model-implied covariance matrix" to the "sample covariance matrix."

### What does "model fit" mean?

Suppose you give the model only certain paths (e.g., T1 loneliness is not allowed to predict T3 depression directly). From these paths, the model "works out" what the covariances among all the variables should be. Compare these with the actual sample covariances: the smaller the difference, the better the model fit. Poor fit means your model has missed important structure in the data.

**Analogy:**

A model is like a "simplified map," and the fit indices tell you "how far the map is from the actual terrain." A map with no simplification at all (a saturated model) always fits perfectly, but it tells you nothing.

### Four common fit indices

| Index | Full name | Intuition | Common cutoff |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| χ² test | chi-square test | Significance test of "the model fits perfectly"; almost always significant in large samples | p > .05 means no significant misfit (of limited use when N is large) |
| CFI / TLI | comparative / Tucker–Lewis fit index | How much better the model is than a baseline model in which no variables are correlated | ≥ .95 good, ≥ .90 acceptable |
| RMSEA | root mean square error of approximation | Average misfit per degree of freedom; takes the model's parsimony into account | ≤ .06 good, > .10 poor |
| SRMR | standardized root mean square residual | Average size of the differences between observed and model-implied correlations | ≤ .08 good |

**Note:**

These cutoffs (often called the "Hu & Bentler criteria") are rules of thumb, not laws. Different indices can contradict each other, and good fit does not mean the model is "right," only that "the data have not clearly rejected it."

### Actual results on the course data

The companion script `R/L5-2.R` gives, on the simulated data with N = 800: χ²(4) = 67.99, p < .001; CFI = .962; TLI = .856; RMSEA = .141; SRMR = .031. The indices disagree: CFI and SRMR look acceptable, but TLI and RMSEA are poor. Why? One important reason is that the simulated data contain strong, stable personal traits, and the standard CLPM has no way to express "a direct similarity between T1 loneliness and T3 loneliness other than through T2." The RI-CLPM in Lesson 5.3 was designed for exactly this, and it fits significantly better.

### Equality constraints: are the paths the same in both intervals?

If T1→T2 and T2→T3 have the same lag and people are at a similar stage of life, you might assume that the autoregressive and cross-lagged paths are equal in the two intervals. This is called an equality constraint. Benefits: fewer parameters, more stable estimates, simpler interpretation ("every six months …"). Cost: if the two intervals really differ, the constraint makes the fit worse. You can compare the "constrained" and "unconstrained" models (a nested-model χ² difference test) and let the data tell you whether the constraint is reasonable.

In lavaan, giving two paths the same "label" constrains them to be equal: `dep2 ~ d*lone1` and `dep3 ~ d*lone2` both use the label `d`.

**Connection to CLPN:**

The simplified "LASSO-only" CLPN that this course teaches as its main approach is not an SEM but a set of separate LASSO regressions, so it has **no** overall fit indices such as CFI or RMSEA; nor does it constrain T1→T2 and T2→T3 to be equal by default. Instead it estimates them separately and compares how well they "replicate" (Lesson 7.5). The full procedure recommended by Wysocki et al. (2025) is different: it refits the model selected by LASSO in lavaan without a penalty, so it **does** have χ², CFI, TLI and RMSEA, and it can test equality over time with nested χ² tests. `R/L7-5b.R` in Lesson 7.5 demonstrates this on the course data: χ²(91) = 164.70, CFI = .988, RMSEA = .032. When you read a CLPN paper, first check which of the two the authors used.

### Common questions and pitfalls

How do you put the difference between between-person and within-person in one sentence?

Between-person asks: "Are people who are lonelier **than others** also more depressed than others?" Within-person asks: "When someone is lonelier **than usual for them**, are they more depressed afterwards?" The first compares different people; the second compares the same person's states at different times. Social work interventions aim at change within individuals, so we usually care more about the within-person question.

If the between-person correlation is positive, must the within-person correlation be positive too?

No. In the interactive figures of Lessons 4.1 and 5.3 you can set the two in opposite directions. A classic example: people who exercise regularly have lower resting heart rates overall (between-person: more exercise ↔ lower heart rate), but while a given person is exercising, their heart rate is higher than usual (within-person: more exercise ↔ higher heart rate). Be very careful when you move from the group level to the individual level, or you commit the "ecological fallacy".

Social Work Connection

Agency evaluation reports often say: "Older adults with higher loneliness scores are also more depressed, so we should reduce loneliness." This sentence mixes two levels. A more accurate version: "The two are correlated across people; whether reducing a particular older adult's loneliness would lower their later depression needs within-person evidence or an intervention study."

## ② Essential Math Write the Intuition as Equations

The core idea of SEM in one mathematical sentence: find the parameters \(\theta\) that make the model-implied covariance matrix \(\Sigma(\theta)\) as close as possible to the sample covariance matrix \(S\).

\[\hat\theta = \arg\min_{\theta}\ F\big(S,\ \Sigma(\theta)\big)\]

| Symbol | Reading / Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| \(\theta\) | Greek letter theta: the set of all parameters to estimate (path coefficients, variances, covariances) |
| \(S\) | Sample covariance matrix: computed directly from the data |
| \(\Sigma(\theta)\) | Capital sigma: the covariance matrix the model "works out" for given parameters |
| \(F(\cdot)\) | Discrepancy function. With maximum likelihood (ML) estimation, it measures the gap between S and Σ(θ) |
| \(\arg\min\) | "The parameter value that makes … smallest" |

#### Where do the degrees of freedom (df) come from?

6 observed variables have \(6\times7/2 = 21\) variances and covariances (plus 6 means = 27 pieces of "known information"). The model estimates 23 parameters (lavaan prints "Number of model parameters 23," means/intercepts included), so \(df = 27 - 23 = 4\). The more df, the more "parsimonious" the model, and the more chances the data have to reject it.

#### The RMSEA formula (for reference)

\[\mathrm{RMSEA}=\sqrt{\max\!\left(\frac{\chi^2-df}{df\,(N-1)},\,0\right)}\]

With χ² = 67.99, df = 4, N = 800: \(\sqrt{(67.99-4)/(4\times799)} = \sqrt{0.0200}\approx 0.141\), matching the output. You can see that RMSEA is large when χ² is much larger than df, and that the larger N is, the smaller the RMSEA for the same χ².

## ③ R Code Implement in R

Core of R/L5-2.R

```r
source("R/_setup.R")
library(lavaan)
dat  <- read_course_data()
wide <- data.frame(id = dat$id)
for (t in 1:3) {                                   # Construct totals (raw scores, not standardized)
  wide[[paste0("lone", t)]] <- rowSums(dat[, paste0(lone_items, "_t", t)])
  wide[[paste0("dep", t)]]  <- rowSums(dat[, paste0(dep_items, "_t", t)])
}
clpm <- '
  lone2 ~ lone1 + dep1      # ~  reads "is predicted by" (regression)
  dep2  ~ dep1  + lone1
  lone3 ~ lone2 + dep2
  dep3  ~ dep2  + lone2
  lone1 ~~ dep1             # ~~ reads "is correlated with" (covariance)
  lone2 ~~ dep2             # At T2 and T3 these are residual covariances
  lone3 ~~ dep3
'
fit <- sem(clpm, data = wide, missing = "fiml")   # FIML: full information maximum likelihood, handles missing data
summary(fit, standardized = TRUE, fit.measures = TRUE)
fitMeasures(fit, c("chisq", "df", "pvalue", "cfi", "tli", "rmsea", "srmr"))
ssol <- standardizedSolution(fit)                 # Standardized estimates + their own SEs and 95% CIs
ssol[ssol$op == "~", c("lhs", "op", "rhs", "est.std", "se", "ci.lower", "ci.upper", "pvalue")]
```

**Reading the output:** the `Regressions:` part of `summary(..., standardized = TRUE)` has a `Std.all` column (fully standardized coefficients), but the `Std.Err` and `P(>|z|)` on the same row, and the `ci.lower` / `ci.upper` from `parameterEstimates()`, all belong to the **unstandardized** estimates (raw-score units). If you put an unstandardized interval next to a standardized coefficient, readers will think "the interval for 0.607 is [0.524, 0.634]," although the two are not on the same scale at all. So when you report β, use `standardizedSolution()`: it recomputes the standard error, z test and 95% CI for the standardized estimate `est.std` with the delta method, so all numbers are on the same scale as β. ### Worked example: from lavaan output to a path table

The output of `summary(fit, standardized = TRUE)` is long. Don't read it from top to bottom. Pull out what you need for a paper in four steps:

1. **Check that estimation ended normally.** The top of the output should say "lavaan ... ended normally after N iterations". If it says "did not converge", or warns about negative variances, none of the numbers below can be used. First check the scales of your variables (if they differ a lot, divide the larger ones by 10), or whether the model is written incorrectly.

2. **Then look at fit (Model Test User Model and fitMeasures).** Note χ², df and p, along with CFI, TLI, RMSEA (with its 90% CI) and SRMR, for the table note.

3. **Next, find the Regressions section.** Each row is one path, for example `dep2 ~ lone1`. Look at `Estimate` (unstandardized), `Std.all` (standardized β) and `P(>|z|)`. Put the 4 autoregressive and 4 cross-lagged paths into one table: paths as rows; b, SE, β and p as columns.

4. **Finally, look at Covariances.** `lone2 ~~ dep2` is the **residual** covariance of the two T2 variables: how much they still vary together at the same time point after the T1 predictors are taken out. It is not simply "the correlation between T2 loneliness and T2 depression".

`parameterEstimates(fit, standardized = TRUE)` turns these results into a data frame, and `subset(pe, op == "~")` keeps only the regression paths. This is far less error-prone than copying numbers off the screen.

**Common mistakes in the code**

- **A misspelled variable name in the model string.** lavaan reports "some observed variables are not found in the data", and the name it gives is the misspelled one. Check each name against `names(dat)`.

- **Leaving out the covariance between the T1 variables.** lavaan's `sem()` estimates the covariances among exogenous (T1) variables by default. But if you write out every parameter yourself with `lavaan()`, you must add `lone1 ~~ dep1`; otherwise the model assumes they are uncorrelated and fits very badly.

- **Comparing two cross-lagged paths by their p-values.** One path with p = .001 and another with p = .20 does not show that the two paths differ significantly. You need an equality constraint and a χ² difference test, as in this lesson.

- **Reporting CFI and leaving out RMSEA.** This lesson's data are a counterexample: CFI looks acceptable, but RMSEA = .141 is poor. Report all the indices and discuss why they disagree.

  lone2 ~~ dep2

cat("Note: CFI >= .95, RMSEA <= .06, and SRMR <= .08 are commonly used benchmarks for \"good fit\".\n")
# Standardized estimates should be paired with standardized confidence intervals:

## ④ Concept Check Instant Check

In lavaan syntax, what do `~` and `~~` mean?

- A. Correlation; regression

- The wrong way round: `~` is regression, `~~` is covariance.

- B. Regression; correlation (covariance) (Correct)

- Correct: `y ~ x` is a regression, `x ~~ y` a covariance.

- C. Latent variable definition; regression

- A latent variable is defined with `=~`, not `~`.

- D. Mean; variance

- A mean is written `x ~ 1` and a variance `x ~~ x`; `~` and `~~` themselves mean regression and covariance.

What does RMSEA = 0.141 tell you?

- A. The model fits very well

- Only RMSEA ≤ .06 is usually considered good, and .141 is above .10.

- B. The model fits poorly (> .10) and has probably missed important structure (Correct)

- Correct; in this data, what was missed is mainly the stable traits (see 5.3).

- C. The cross-lagged paths are significant

- Overall fit indices have nothing to do with whether a single path is significant.

- D. The sample is too small

- N = 800 is not small; and the larger N is, the smaller the RMSEA for the same χ².

CFI = .962 but TLI = .856. Which statement is correct?

- A. Looking at CFI alone is enough

- Picking only the index that suits you hides the model's problems; here RMSEA is also poor.

- B. When indices disagree, weigh them together and think about what the model may be missing (Correct)

- Correct.

- C. TLI must have been computed wrongly

- TLI penalizes model complexity more, so it often differs from CFI; that does not mean a calculation error.

- D. It shows that the cross-lagged paths are 0

- Overall fit indices do not test whether any single path is 0.

What is the main reason for putting an "equality constraint" on matching paths in T1→T2 and T2→T3?

- A. To make the p values smaller

- Constraints are for parsimony and stability; choosing a model to get smaller p values is selective reporting.

- B. When the lags and settings are similar, it is more parsimonious and more stable, and a model comparison can test whether it is reasonable (Correct)

- Correct.

- C. lavaan requires it

- By default lavaan estimates every path freely; whether to constrain is the researcher's decision, and it should be tested.

- D. It guarantees a larger CFI

- A constraint removes free parameters, so the fit usually gets slightly worse or stays the same; CFI does not necessarily increase.

Many CLPN papers don't report CFI / RMSEA. What is the most likely reason?

- A. Item-level data have no covariance matrix

- Items have a covariance matrix too; the SEM refit in the full procedure computes fit indices at exactly the item level.

- B. They use the simplified "LASSO-only" version: separate nodewise LASSO regressions, with no re-estimation of the model as one SEM, so there are no overall fit indices; papers that use the full procedure of Wysocki et al. (2025) (refitting in lavaan) do report χ² / CFI / TLI / RMSEA (Correct)

- Correct. The simplified version chooses λ by cross-validation / EBIC and assesses stability with the bootstrap; the full procedure has overall fit indices after the SEM refit and can test equality over time with nested χ² tests (Lesson 7.5, R/L7-5b.R).

- C. A CLPN always fits perfectly

- Separate nodewise LASSO regressions have no overall fit test, so "always perfect" makes no sense; and the fit after the full procedure's refit can also be poor.

- D. No CLPN can ever have fit indices

- The SEM refit in the full procedure does give these indices (7.5).

#### Exercise 2: RMSEA and degrees of freedom by handUnderstanding

(1) Explain why this model has df = 4. (2) If the same model gave the same χ² = 67.99 in a sample of N = 200, what would the RMSEA be? What does that show?

Show Reference Solution / Walkthrough

(1) 6 variables give 21 variances/covariances + 6 means = 27 pieces of information; the model estimates 23 parameters (6 intercepts/means, 8 regressions, 2 T1 variances, 4 residual variances, 3 covariances), so 27 − 23 = 4. (2) \(\sqrt{63.99/(4\times199)}\approx 0.284\). The same χ² means much worse misfit in a small sample; conversely, χ² easily becomes significant in a large sample, so you can't rely on the p value of χ² alone.

## Summary Key Takeaways & Further Reading

- SEM fit = how close the model-implied covariance Σ(θ) is to the sample covariance S.

- Common indices: χ², CFI/TLI (≥ .95), RMSEA (≤ .06), SRMR (≤ .08); these are rules of thumb and must be weighed together.

- The CLPM on the course data: RMSEA = .141, TLI = .856, a sign that stable traits were omitted.

- Equality constraint = give paths the same label; a nested-model χ² difference test shows whether it is reasonable.

- The LASSO-only CLPN has no overall fit indices; it "makes its case" through the choice of λ and bootstrap stability. (The full procedure of Wysocki et al., 2025, does give fit indices after its unpenalized SEM refit.)
