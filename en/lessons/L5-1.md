## ① Intuition Build the Mental Picture

### A social work scenario

You work at a community service center. Three times, six months apart, you ask the same group of residents to fill in a loneliness scale and a depression scale. The center's director asks you: "Is it loneliness that makes people depressed, or depression that makes people lonelier and lonelier?" Questions of "which comes first" like this are what the cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) tries to answer.

The idea of the CLPM can be broken into three statements:

1. **People have inertia.** Someone who was very lonely six months ago will probably still be fairly lonely six months later. This "predicting yourself from yourself" arrow is called an autoregressive path (AR); it stands for the stability of individual differences.

2. **After removing the inertia, see whether the other variable adds anything.** If, once you know a person's depression six months ago, also knowing their loneliness six months ago predicts their current depression better, then the cross-lagged path (CL) "loneliness T1 → depression T2" is not 0.

3. **At the same time point, the two move together anyway.** The correlation between loneliness and depression within one measurement occasion (and, at T2 and T3, the correlation between "the parts not explained by the previous wave") is shown as a within-wave correlation / residual covariance, drawn as a curved double-headed arrow.

**Key intuition:**

A cross-lagged coefficient does not answer "are loneliness and depression related?" It answers "given your past depression, does your past loneliness predict your future depression any better?" That is what "controlling for the autoregressive path" means.

### Path diagrams: the visual language of SEM

The CLPM is a structural equation model (SEM). SEM usually expresses a model as a path diagram, and the conventions are few:

| Symbol | Meaning | In the CLPM |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Box □ | Observed variable (a score measured directly) | Loneliness T1, depression T2, … (here, scale totals) |
| Ellipse ○ | Latent variable (cannot be measured directly) | The "random intercepts" of Lesson 5.3 are latent variables |
| Single-headed arrow → | Regression (prediction) path | Autoregressive paths a, b; cross-lagged paths c, d |
| Curved double-headed arrow ↔ | Correlation / covariance | (Residual) correlation within a wave |

### Why must a cross-lagged path "control for" the autoregressive path?

Suppose loneliness and depression are already strongly correlated at T1 (say r = .5), and each is very stable. Even if loneliness adds nothing at all to the prediction of depression, "loneliness T1" and "depression T2" will still be quite strongly correlated, because loneliness T1 is correlated with depression T1, and depression T1 strongly predicts depression T2. If depression T1 is left out of the equation, the path "loneliness T1 → depression T2" takes credit for this "borrowed" correlation. This is the same omitted-variable bias you met in Module 3 (leave out a variable that is correlated with the predictor and also affects the outcome, and the coefficient "absorbs" its effect; here it is inflated; review [3.2 Multiple Regression and 'Controlling for Other Variables'](L3-2.html)).

### If c is significant and d is not, what does that tell you?

Suppose "depression T1 → loneliness T2" (c) is significant and "loneliness T1 → depression T2" (d) is not. The most careful wording is: *in this sample and at this lag, people with higher depression at T1 also had higher loneliness at T2, after controlling for T1 loneliness; the prediction in the other direction was not supported.* Note three things: (1) "not significant" does not mean "absent"; the sample size or the lag may simply be unsuitable; (2) one path significant and the other not does not mean the two "differ significantly"; comparing them needs its own test (e.g., constrain c and d to be equal in lavaan and compare the models); (3) this is still prediction over time, not causation (see [5.4 From Prediction to Causality: What Longitudinal Models Can and Cannot Claim](L5-4.html)).

### CLPM vs. CLPN: from "total scores" to "items"

In a traditional CLPM, each box is the total score of a construct (e.g., the sum of the 4 loneliness items), so all you can say is that "loneliness as a whole" predicts "depression as a whole." But in social work practice you would rather know: is it the feeling that "no one understands me," or the situation of "lacking companionship," that better predicts later "loss of interest"? CLPN simply replaces each box of the CLPM with a single item, so the 4 paths between 2 variables become 81 paths among 9 items (9 autoregressive + 72 cross-lagged). With that many paths, you need LASSO to "pick" among them ([3.5 Overfitting and Regularization: Ridge, LASSO, and Cross-Validation](L3-5.html) in Module 3, and Module 7).

**A reminder:**

This lesson's R script first uses ordinary regression, `lm()`, to "approximate" one time interval of the CLPM (T1→T2), so you can see that "each T2 variable is one regression equation." The next lesson, 5.2, fits the whole three-wave model in one go with lavaan.

## ② Essential Math Write the Intuition as Equations

The math of the CLPM is just two multiple regression equations (per time interval). Assume all variables have been standardized (z scores, mean 0, standard deviation 1; see Module 1):

\[\text{Loneliness}_{t+1} = a\cdot \text{Loneliness}_{t} + c\cdot \text{Depression}_{t} + e^{L}_{t+1}\]

\[\text{Depression}_{t+1} = b\cdot \text{Depression}_{t} + d\cdot \text{Loneliness}_{t} + e^{D}_{t+1}\]

| Symbol | Reading / Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| \(t,\ t+1\) | Two adjacent measurement occasions (waves), e.g., T1 and T2 |
| \(a,\ b\) | Autoregressive coefficients: how strongly "your own past" predicts "your own present," controlling for the other variable. Standardized, they are usually between 0.3 and 0.8 |
| \(c,\ d\) | Cross-lagged coefficients: how much "the other variable's past" adds to the prediction of "your own present," controlling for your own past. c = depression → loneliness, d = loneliness → depression |
| \(e^{L}_{t+1},\ e^{D}_{t+1}\) | Residuals: the part the equation does not explain. The superscripts L and D are just labels for the loneliness and depression equations |
| \(\mathrm{Cov}(e^{L}_{t+1}, e^{D}_{t+1})\) | The covariance of the two residuals in the same wave: the curved double-headed arrows at T2 and T3 in the path diagram. It stands for "shared change that happens at the same time but cannot be explained by the previous wave" |

#### How do you read a standardized coefficient?

If \(d = 0.13\), read it as: *of two people with the same T1 depression, the one whose T1 loneliness is 1 standard deviation higher has a predicted T2 depression that is on average 0.13 standard deviations higher.* "Of two people with the same …" is the plain-language version of "controlling for."

#### How many paths do three waves have?

Two variables, three waves: T1→T2 has 2 autoregressive + 2 cross-lagged paths, T2→T3 another 4, so 8 regression paths in all; add the T1 correlation and the T2 and T3 residual correlations, 3 double-headed arrows. In general: with k variables, each time interval needs k equations, each with k predictors, so each interval has \(k \times k\) coefficients. That is where the \(p\times p\) coefficient matrix of a CLPN comes from.

**Connection to calculus:**

If you remember partial derivatives, the cross-lagged coefficient \(d\) is \(\partial\,\mathbb{E}[\text{Depression}_{t+1}] / \partial\,\text{Loneliness}_t\): hold \(\text{Depression}_t\) fixed and let only \(\text{Loneliness}_t\) change by one unit; how much does the prediction change? If you don't remember, that's fine: "holding the other variables constant" is all it means.

## ③ R Code Implement in R

Every course script starts with `source("R/_setup.R")`: it sets the working directory to the course folder, defines the item names `lone_items` (4 loneliness items) and `dep_items` (5 depression items), and provides the data-loading function `read_course_data()`. The data, `data/clpn_sim_wide.csv`, are a simulated three-wave panel with N = 800; variable names look like `lone1_t1` (loneliness item 1, wave 1).

Core of R/L5-1.R (with line-by-line comments)

```r
source("R/_setup.R")            # Shared setup: paths, item names, fonts, helper functions
dat <- read_course_data()       # Read data/clpn_sim_wide.csv (N = 800)
z <- function(x) as.numeric(scale(x))   # Standardize to z scores
for (t in 1:3) {                # Total score of each construct at each wave, standardized
  dat[[paste0("lone", t)]] <- z(rowSums(dat[, paste0(lone_items, "_t", t)]))
  dat[[paste0("dep", t)]]  <- z(rowSums(dat[, paste0(dep_items, "_t", t)]))
}
# Each T2 variable = one regression equation
coef(lm(lone2 ~ lone1 + dep1, data = dat))   # a (lone1) and c (dep1)
coef(lm(dep2  ~ dep1 + lone1, data = dat))   # b (dep1) and d (lone1)
```

**How to read it:** `paste0(lone_items, "_t", t)` builds column names such as `"lone1_t1" "lone2_t1" …`; `rowSums()` adds up each person's 4 loneliness items; `lm(y ~ x1 + x2)` is the multiple regression from Module 3, and the tilde `~` reads "is predicted by."

**Actual results on the course data:**`lone2 ~ lone1 + dep1` gives a ≈ 0.607, c ≈ 0.016; `dep2 ~ dep1 + lone1` gives b ≈ 0.539, d ≈ 0.128. So in this simulated data, "loneliness T1 → depression T2" is clearly larger than the reverse. (These numbers are exactly the same as lavaan's standardized results in the next lesson, because the regression part of a one-interval CLPM is just two regressions.)

## ④ Concept Check Instant Check

In a CLPM, what is the path "depression T1 → depression T2" called?

- A. Cross-lagged path

- A cross-lagged path connects different variables at different times; this path is a variable predicting itself.

- B. Autoregressive path (Correct)

- Autoregressive = a variable predicting itself; it stands for stability.

- C. Within-wave correlation

- A within-wave correlation connects two variables in the same wave and is a double-headed arrow.

- D. Residual

- A residual is the part an equation does not explain, not a path.

Why must a cross-lagged path control for the autoregressive path?

- A. To make the model fit look better

- The control removes "borrowed" association; it has nothing to do with how good the fit indices look.

- B. Because the two variables are correlated at T1 and each is stable; without the control, the cross-lagged path "borrows" the association that stability produces (Correct)

- Correct: at heart this is omitted-variable bias.

- C. Because lavaan requires it

- lavaan lets you leave out the autoregressive paths; the control is required by the statistical logic, not by the software.

- D. To turn the coefficients into standardized coefficients

- Standardization only changes units; it has nothing to do with which variables are controlled in the equation.

c (depression → loneliness) is significant and d (loneliness → depression) is not. What is the most appropriate conclusion?

- A. Depression causes loneliness

- A CLPM only gives evidence of prediction over time; it cannot say "causes" directly.

- B. Loneliness does not affect depression

- Not significant ≠ absent: the sample size or the lag may simply be unsuitable.

- C. In this sample and at this lag, T1 depression adds to the prediction of T2 loneliness; the other direction was not supported, and the difference between the two needs its own test (Correct)

- Correct: careful wording, and it notes that "one significant, one not" is not the same as "significantly different."

- D. The effects in the two directions differ significantly

- One path significant and the other not does not show that they differ significantly; you need a constraint test to conclude that.

What does the curved double-headed arrow ↔ mean in a path diagram?

- A. Regression

- Regression (prediction) is a single-headed arrow.

- B. Correlation / covariance (Correct)

- Correct.

- C. Causation

- Path diagrams have no special symbol for "causation"; a single-headed arrow also means only regression.

- D. Latent variable

- A latent variable is drawn as an ellipse, not as a kind of arrow.

What is the main difference between a CLPM and a CLPN?

- A. A CLPM doesn't need longitudinal data

- Both need panel data from the same people.

- B. The variables of a CLPM are construct total scores; the nodes of a CLPN are single items / symptoms (Correct)

- Correct: a CLPN takes the CLPM "down to the item level."

- C. A CLPN has no autoregressive paths

- A CLPN has autoregressive paths too: the diagonal of the coefficient matrix holds the autoregressive edges.

- D. A CLPN can only use two time points

- A CLPN can use more than two waves (Lesson 7.5).

On paper, draw the full path diagram for three waves of "loneliness ↔ depression," label a, b, c, d (you can write a₁, a₂, … for the two intervals), and explain each kind of path in one sentence of social work language.

Show Reference Solution / Walkthrough

There should be 6 boxes (loneliness T1/T2/T3, depression T1/T2/T3); 4 horizontal autoregressive arrows; 4 diagonal cross-lagged arrows; and 3 curved double-headed arrows (the T1 correlation and the T2 and T3 residual correlations).

- **Autoregressive:** "Residents who were lonelier than others six months ago are usually still lonelier than others six months later."

- **Cross-lagged (loneliness → depression):** "Among residents who were equally depressed six months ago, those who were lonelier then have higher depression scores six months later."

- **Residual correlation:** "Over the same period, people whose loneliness rose more than expected also tended to have depression that rose more than expected."

#### Exercise 2: What happens if you leave out the autoregressive pathUnderstanding

In R, compare the coefficient of `lone1` in `lm(dep2 ~ lone1, dat)` and in `lm(dep2 ~ dep1 + lone1, dat)`. Why is the first one so much larger?

Show Reference Solution / Walkthrough

In the first model, the coefficient of `lone1` is close to "the correlation of loneliness T1 with depression T2," about 0.30 in this data (standardized and with a single predictor, the coefficient equals the correlation r(loneliness T1, depression T2) = 0.296, as in Lesson 4.3); with `dep1` added it drops to about 0.13. The reason: loneliness T1 is correlated with depression T1, and depression T1 strongly predicts depression T2. Without controlling for `dep1`, all of this indirect, "borrowed" association is credited to `lone1`. That is why a cross-lagged path must control for the autoregressive path.

## Summary Key Takeaways & Further Reading

- CLPM = one multiple regression equation for each variable at the later wave; autoregressive paths stand for stability, and cross-lagged paths for "how much the other variable's past adds, once your own past is controlled."

- Path diagram symbols: box = observed variable, ellipse = latent variable, single arrow = regression, double arrow = correlation.

- A cross-lagged path must control for the autoregressive path; otherwise it picks up "borrowed" association from the T1 correlation and from stability.

- A CLPN breaks the total-score boxes of a CLPM into item nodes: p items → p × p coefficients.
